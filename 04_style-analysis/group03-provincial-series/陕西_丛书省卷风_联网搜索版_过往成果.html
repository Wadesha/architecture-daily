西安化觉巷清真寺

Huajue Lane Mosque, Xi'an

级别：国家级重点文物保护单位（第三批） 年代：始建于唐天宝元年（742），现存为明清建筑 地址：西安市莲湖区化觉巷30号 类型：伊斯兰教建筑·中国宫殿式木构

化觉巷清真寺又名东大寺、化觉寺，位于西安西大街鼓楼西北隅的化觉巷内，与大学习巷清真寺东西遥遥相对，因在后者以东，故称东大寺。它是国内占地规模最大、保存最完整的中国宫殿式清真寺，也是西安地区现存规模最大、保存最完整的明代建筑群。与多数中国清真寺一样，它坐西朝东——因为穆斯林礼拜须朝向麦加的克尔白，而麦加正在中国的西面。这一朝向并非只是仪式习惯，它决定了整座寺院自东向西层层递进的格局：信众由东端进入，一路穿门过院，身体的行进方向本身就是朝向圣地的方向。寺院东西长约二百四十五至二百五十米，南北宽四十七至八十米，占地约一点三万平方米（一说一点二万平方米），建筑面积约六千平方米（一说四千平方米），殿、楼、亭、台、厅、堂共一百八十余间，被联合国教科文组织列为世界伊斯兰文物之一。一九八八年一月十三日由国务院公布为第三批全国重点文物保护单位。

关于始建年代，历来聚讼。寺内《创建清真寺碑记》称建于唐天宝元年（742），撰文者为赐进士及第、户部员外郎兼侍御史王鉷，碑文以"千圣一心，万古一理"立论，比附尧之"钦若昊天"、汤之"圣敬日跻"、文王之"昭事上帝"、孔子之"获罪于天，无所祷"，论证伊斯兰之道与中国圣人之道同源。但学者多以现存实物为据，认为寺内确有唐代遗存（省心楼一层地面所嵌"二龙戏珠"石刻相传即唐代物），却难证明唐代已有完整寺院；亦有据长江考古队式的柱础分析提出始建于明洪武年间者。可以确考的修葺脉络是：明洪武十七年（1384），朱元璋特许兵部尚书铁铉重修与扩建；洪武二十五年（1392）创寺敕命碑记其事；明成化二年（1466）重修，十八年（1482）奏请改寺名为"敕赐清修寺"；嘉靖元年至五年（1522—1526）重修时"充拓门殿，规模广远，栋宇高耸，较前若倍"；万历三十四年（1606）大修；清乾隆年间（一说乾隆三十年1765、一说三十三年1768）的重修扩建形成今日格局；民国三十六年（1947）又重修。据《敕赐清修寺重修碑记》载，全寺所用的琉璃瓦与瓦当吻兽，原为明代皇家宫殿建筑的特有建材，系嘉靖皇帝特许使用——这解释了为何一座清真寺可以覆孔雀蓝、天蓝色琉璃瓦。二〇〇八年，地震受损建筑获保护性修复；二〇二一年，南宾厅修缮项目开工。

东端院墙正中的照壁是全寺中轴线的起点，也构成了中式"藏风聚气"的空间过渡——照壁不直接开门，须从两侧绕行。照壁为仿木青砖结构，歇山顶，覆孔雀蓝琉璃瓦，檐下有精致砖构斗拱，壁心砖雕为三方菱形菊纹（一作菊莲图），周边饰缠枝莲、牡丹等图案，线条流畅、层次分明，据证为清代所筑，是西安地区体量最大的砖雕照壁之一。照壁对面，第一进院中心耸立着三间四柱三楼重檐歇山顶的木牌楼，清康熙年间建，是西安现存两座古牌楼之一，通体木构榫卯相接、无钉无铁，额枋与雀替满雕龙凤、卷草纹样，屋面覆琉璃瓦，翼角飞翘。其东面书"敕赐礼拜寺"，为明代书画家董其昌手迹，落款明天启六年（1626）；西面镌"道法参天地"，为宋代书法家米芾手迹，落款宋元丰年间。牌楼高度有九米与十七米两说并存。牌楼之后是"五间楼"，因面阔五间、进深两间、硬山穿堂式而得名，从功能上讲它才是清真寺真正的大门，建筑规格颇高，门前列石鼓三对，两侧连接东西的铺砖甬道，明代嘉靖年间重修时定型。院南北两侧各有厢房三间，现陈列明清古式家具。

第二进院中心立明代三间四柱式石牌坊，中楣镌"天监在兹"，两侧分刻"钦翼昭事"与"虔诚省礼"，东西有踏道，绕以石雕栏杆。"天监在兹"四字化用了《诗经》：既取《大雅·大明》"天监在此，有命既集"，又取《周颂·敬之》"陟降厥士，日监在兹"——古汉语中"兹""此"通用，合起来意为上天正监视着我们在此地此刻的言行；"钦翼昭事"出自《大雅·大明》"维此文王，小心翼翼，昭事上帝"。用儒家经典中最庄重的语句来表达伊斯兰的信仰，是这座寺院最耐人寻味之处。牌坊坊柱与额枋遍饰线刻、透雕，图案有缠枝莲、云纹、瑞兽。坊后南北两侧各竖冲天雕龙碑一通，均为龙首、形制高大：北侧为明万历三十四年（1606）四月所立《敕赐重修清真寺碑》，文为冯从吾所撰，碑阴镌米芾手书"道法参天地"五个大字；南侧为清乾隆三十三年（1768）所立《敕修清真寺碑》，碑阴镌明天启年间礼部侍郎董其昌手书"敕赐礼拜寺"五个大字。有意思的是，"道法"二字源出老子《道德经》"道法自然"，在清真寺题字中使用道家核心语汇，体现的是"道伊同理"的调和思想。

第三进院入口处的敕修殿（又作敕赐殿）是寺内年代最早的殿宇，面阔三间，单檐歇山式，朱门金钉、砖雕银饰，前檐悬挂董其昌题"敕赐礼拜寺"匾额，殿名亦由此而来。殿内中央铺"二龙戏珠"石面一幅，南北立碑碣六块，其中最著名的是清雍正十年（1732）三月十五日所立的阿拉伯文"月碑"，撰文者为时任清真大寺阿訇、人称"小西宁"的伊斯兰教学者，内容是推算伊斯兰教斋月出入月的方法。他引经据典，试图弥合当时坊民在进出斋月上的分歧，勒石立于寺中正是要让坊民明了宗教依据。此外还有明景泰六年（1455）李谦撰文的《重修礼拜寺无相记》。殿内西向悬挂慈禧太后手书"派衍天方"匾，东向悬挂"古教清真"匾——这两方匾的来历颇为具体：清光绪二十六年（1900）庚子事变，八国联军攻入北京，慈禧偕光绪帝"西狩"西安；第二年还都之日，为感谢民众与神灵庇佑，给西安的寺、庙、观、庵题字悬匾，经办此事的是前署臬司唐冕周，送给化觉巷清真大寺的两方即慈禧的"派衍天方"与光绪的"教崇西域"。院中心的中轴线上矗立省心楼，即邦克楼（宣礼塔），三层八角攒尖顶砖木结构，高约二十米（另有二层三檐八角攒尖顶之说），层层收分，檐角挂风铃，顶覆蓝色琉璃瓦。楼内屋架以木构件层层叠架，**无立柱支撑**，为中式楼阁中的罕例。"省心"之名，取自"三省吾身"——深受宋明理学影响，唤身不如唤心。楼南北两侧为阿訇住所，因日常迎送往来，坊间称"南北官殿"：南侧官殿陈设富丽，正中悬挂苏轼、米芾、岳飞、黄庭坚四幅竹皮镶嵌书法条幅，装置有长安八景图案的格扇；北侧讲经堂珍藏明代手抄本《古兰经》与清代绘制的《麦加图》。

第四进院大门俗称"连三门"，满花砖雕，堪称珍品；门额有阿拉伯文三则，中间为"奉至仁至慈的真主之名"，南向为"万物非主，唯有真主，穆罕默德，主之使者"，北向为"清真寺是敬畏者的家"。院中心的一真亭又称凤凰亭，集碑楼与亭阁于一体：中央为六角攒尖顶主亭，两侧各一三角形翼亭，三亭相连，状如凤凰展翅。亭中西向悬"一真"雕龙小匾，为明建文元年（1399）兵部尚书铁铉（一作铁玄）手书；东向牌匾"包罗宇宙"语出《古兰经》。亭中两边瓦牌上是一副三十字的对联："品尊至圣不帝不王悦服帝王者万国；行注真经为道为教景从道教于四方"，言简意赅地概括了先知穆罕默德的生平与大德。亭南、北两侧各有面阔七间的厅堂：南厅原为掌教阿訇接待历代朝廷文武官员宣谕圣旨的官厅，厅内展有明代桌椅、瓷器、字画，其中最珍贵的是十二块黄杨木寿屏——高四米半，宽六十厘米，光滑如绸缎，以百宝镶嵌，图案各异，乃清康熙帝的赐物，其中一块上镶有一只玉质蜜蜂，这十二块寿屏在国内亦属罕见；北厅陈设唐天宝元年《创建清真寺碑记》、明嘉靖《重修清真寺》碑，以及清官石、日晷等石作。亭后有海棠形鱼池两个，碧水清波，池畔各有四角攒尖顶碑亭一座。院西侧为高大的月台，面积约六百五十二平方米，四周石栏环绕；月台西面立三座石牌坊（一说东侧立三座石门），中门御道正中有盘龙戏珠石雕，台西端即礼拜大殿。宽大的月台上还点缀着一批历代石作以装点空间：隋唐时期的长明灯，被称为"吉星高照"；唐宋时期的白莲花座；明代的莲升三级座；以及古石盆、老鼠石、雪坡石等。台上另陈有石日晷，和一通钉满铁钉的"试官石"。殿前北侧有一木制小楼阁，名呼图拜楼，是领拜者诵祷词的地方。

礼拜大殿是全寺最大的建筑物，始建于明洪武二十五年（1392），平面呈"凸"字形，面阔七间、宽三十二点九五米，进深九间、三十八点五三米，总面积近一千三百平方米，由两座单檐歇山顶勾连搭而成，锥形镏金顶，明柱雕花隔扇殿门；因其为"敕建"，故覆孔雀蓝（一说天蓝）琉璃瓦，大殿"稳兽"由明秦王府承举正"启秦王殿下赐之"。大殿可容千人礼拜（一说二千人）。殿前两侧内山墙为巨幅砖雕花卉硕果，雕工精细、富于质感，为清代砖雕精品。相邻檐柱用双柱，上做南北排水天沟。梁架做法近宋代殿堂遗制：金柱不升高，梁柱交点仍用斗拱，似保持明代原状；梁架下做天花；后部虎尾与前部搭接，在前部后檐采用大额做法，以大额承受梁架，以适应虎尾与前部梁柱不对缝的情况。外檐斗拱为五踩重昂制式。殿内最震撼的是藻井天花，共六百余幅彩画，均为阿拉伯文与蔓草纹组成的团花图案，中心为阿文经文，形似篆体，构图着色各有千秋——这是中国伊斯兰教寺院彩画的独特手法；大殿四周另有全版木刻《古兰经》。殿内的匾额层层叠叠：正面檐下悬"泰斯米"（意为"奉至仁至慈的真主之名"），其左右陆续恢复增加六方形状不一、颜色各异的匾额，从右至左为"永信独一""普慈万民""惟精惟一""为善最乐""本然清高""道冠古今"；内檐下悬"临下有赫"，语出《诗经·大雅·皇矣》；二进悬"能有有有"匾，概述真主的原造之能与被造物的再造之力——三个"有"中间为楷书、两边一笔写就，以此告知先天与后天的"有"是连续而未知的；四进悬"钦若昊天"，语出《诗经·大雅》。明柱上另悬八十字的瓦形牌长联，以"独""无"二字称颂安拉的德性。大殿后部接深广各三间的后窑殿，屋面亦覆孔雀蓝琉璃瓦，西壁有一凹壁，即"米哈拉布"（圣龛），是阿訇带领信众礼拜之处，也是教徒朝拜的方向——伊斯兰教反对偶像崇拜，故礼拜殿前方空无一物，仅做朝向识别而已。凹壁周围以满花图案与库法体经文饰满墙壁。殿前左侧为宣谕台，供阿訇说教时站立；北侧有一木制小楼阁名"呼图拜楼"，是领拜者诵祷词的地方。大殿两侧设月洞门，可通至后院，为阿訇墓地。

整座寺院是以中华民族传统建筑的布局为基础、兼顾伊斯兰教寺院特色的中国式伊斯兰教建筑群。它的深邃之处在于用中国建筑的语法讲伊斯兰的教义：中轴对称的"一"，诠释的是认主独一；南北对称的布局，诠释的是"认"与"行"两条路径；沿中轴线的匾额取自《古兰经》中关于真主的表述，南北两侧的匾额楹联则围绕先知的圣行、圣品与中国文化对伊斯兰的解读。而信众从东端进入，一路经过"天监在兹"的警醒、"省心"的自省、"奉至仁至慈的真主之名"的洁净提醒，直到月台前"每逢礼拜，你们必须穿着服饰"的衣着提示——建筑空间的序列，本身就是一套引导心灵由喧嚣尘世渐入礼拜状态的设计。一九九七年五月，它被评选为西安市旅游十大景观之一。

The Huajue Lane Mosque, also called the Eastern Great Mosque (Dongda Si) or Huajue Temple, stands in Huajue Lane northwest of the Drum Tower on Xi'an's West Street, facing the Daxuexi Lane Mosque across the city; being the eastern of the two, it is known as the Eastern Great Mosque. It is the largest and best-preserved Chinese palace-style mosque in the country, and the largest and most intact surviving Ming-dynasty building complex in Xi'an. Like most Chinese mosques it faces east — for Muslim prayer must be directed toward the Kaaba in Mecca, which lies west of China. This orientation is more than ritual convention: it determines the temple's westward sequence of courtyards, so that the worshipper's physical progress from the eastern gate is itself a movement toward the holy city. The compound runs some 245–250 meters east to west and 47–80 meters north to south, covering about 13,000 square meters (one source gives 12,000) with roughly 6,000 square meters of floor space (another gives 4,000), and more than 180 halls, towers, pavilions, terraces, and chambers. UNESCO has listed it among the world's Islamic monuments. On 13 January 1988 the State Council proclaimed it a National Key Protected Heritage Site in the third batch.

Its founding date is disputed. The Stele Recording the Founding of the Mosque, composed by Wang Hong — a presented scholar, vice-director of the Ministry of Revenue, and investigating censor — dates it to the first year of Tianbao, Tang (742). The text argues from the premise that "a thousand sages share one mind, and through ten thousand ages one principle," comparing Islam to Yao's "reverently following the august heaven," Tang's "sage reverence daily advancing," King Wen's "brightly serving God on high," and Confucius's "having offended Heaven, there is nowhere to pray," concluding that the Islamic way and the way of China's sages share a single source. Scholars, however, largely rely on surviving fabric: the temple does hold Tang-dynasty relics — the "two dragons playing with a pearl" stone panel set into the floor of the Exhortation Tower's ground level is held to be Tang — but these hardly prove a complete Tang temple; analysis of column bases has also been advanced to support a Hongwu-era founding. What can be documented is the sequence of repairs: in the seventeenth year of Hongwu (1384) the Hongwu emperor specially authorized the Minister of War Tie Xuan to rebuild and expand it; a stele of imperial command dated the twenty-fifth year of Hongwu (1392) records the founding; repairs in the second year of Chenghua (1466) and, in the eighteenth year (1482), a successful petition to rename the temple "Imperially Bestowed Temple of Pure Cultivation"; rebuilding in the first to fifth years of Jiajing (1522–1526) that "expanded the gate and hall, vastly enlarged the scale, and raised theridgepole, doubling what had stood before"; a major overhaul in the thirty-fourth year of Wanli (1606); and Qing reconstruction under Qianlong (given variously as 1765 or 1768) that produced the present layout, followed by further repairs in the thirty-sixth year of the Republic (1947). According to the Stele Recording the Restoration of the Imperially Bestowed Temple of Pure Cultivation, the glazed tiles and roof ornaments used throughout were originally building materials reserved for Ming imperial palaces, permitted by special grant of the Jiajing emperor — which explains how a mosque came to be roofed in peacock-blue and sky-blue glazed tile. In 2008 earthquake-damaged structures underwent protective restoration; in 2021 work began on the south reception hall.

The screen wall at the center of the eastern enclosure wall marks the start of the central axis and provides a Chinese-style transition of "gathering wind and accumulating qi" — the wall has no direct opening, so one must pass around either side. It is of imitation-timber grey brick with a hip-gable roof in peacock-blue glazed tile, refined brick bracket sets beneath the eaves, and a central panel of three lozenge-shaped chrysanthemum medallions (one source gives chrysanthemum-and-lotus) bordered by twining lotus and peony in fluid, clearly layered relief; evidenced as Qing work, it is among the largest brick screen walls in Xi'an. Opposite it, at the center of the first court, rises a three-bay, four-post, three-tier timber gate-tower with a double-eave hip-gable roof, built in the Kangxi era and one of only two ancient gate-towers surviving in Xi'an. Its frame is entirely mortise-and-tenon, without nail or iron; architraves and brackets are carved all over with dragons, phoenixes, and scrolling vines; the roof is glazed and its corner eaves flare upward. Its eastern face reads "Imperially Bestowed Temple of Worship" in the hand of the Ming calligrapher Dong Qichang, dated the sixth year of Tianqi (1626); its western face carries "The Way Models Itself on Heaven and Earth" by the Song calligrapher Mi Fu, dated the Yuanfeng era. The tower's height is given variously as 9 and 17 meters. Behind it stands the "Five-Bay Building," named for its five-bay width, two-bay depth, and flush-gable hall form; functionally it is the mosque's true gate, built to a high specification with three pairs of stone drums set before it, flanked by brick-paved passageways running east and west, and fixed in its present form during the Jiajing rebuilding. Three chambers stand on either side of the court, now displaying Ming and Qing furniture.

At the center of the second court stands a Ming three-bay, four-post stone archway. Its central lintel reads "Heaven Is Watching Here"; the flanking lintels read "Reverently and Carefully Serving" and "Devoutly Examining Oneself in Rites." Steps lie to east and west, enclosed by carved stone balustrades. "Heaven Is Watching Here" is drawn from the Book of Songs, combining "Heaven watches from here; the mandate is already gathered" (Daya · Daming) with "He ascends and descends among his officers, daily watching here" (Zhousong · Jingzhi); in archaic Chinese zi and ci were interchangeable, so the phrase means Heaven is even now watching our conduct in this place at this hour. "Reverently and Carefully Serving" likewise comes from Daming: "This King Wen, cautious and careful, brightly served God on high, and so embraced abundant blessing." That the most solemn language of the Confucian canon should be used to articulate Islamic belief is among the temple's most thought-provoking features. Posts and lintels are covered with incised and openwork carving — twining lotus, cloud scrolls, auspicious beasts. Behind the archway, north and south, stand two soaring dragon-carved steles, both dragon-headed and imposing. The northern one, the Stele of the Imperially Bestowed Reconstruction of the Mosque, was erected in the fourth month of the thirty-fourth year of Wanli (1606), its text composed by Feng Congwu; its reverse carries Mi Fu's five great characters "The Way Models Itself on Heaven and Earth." The southern, the Stele of the Imperially Commissioned Restoration of the Mosque, dates to the thirty-third year of Qianlong (1768), its reverse inscribed with five characters by Dong Qichang, Vice-Minister of Rites in the Tianqi era: "Imperially Bestowed Temple of Worship." Notably,dao fa derives from Laozi's Daodejing — "the Way models itself on nature" — and the use of a core Daoist term within a mosque expresses an intentional harmony of Daoist and Islamic principle.

The Hall of Imperial Restoration (Chixiu Dian, also Chi Ci Dian) at the entrance to the third court is the oldest hall in the temple: three bays wide, single-eave hip-gable, with vermilion doors and gilt studs and silver-toned brick carving; Dong Qichang's plaque "Imperially Bestowed Temple of Worship" hangs at the front eave, from which the hall takes its name. At its center lies a "two dragons playing with a pearl" stone panel, and six steles stand to north and south. The most celebrated is the Arabic "Moon Stele," erected on the fifteenth day of the third month of the tenth year of Yongzheng (1732); its author, the mosque's imam known as "Little Xining," sets out the method for calculating the beginning and end of the Ramadan month. He cited scripture copiously in an attempt to reconcile differences among the community over when the fast began and ended, and the stone was erected precisely so that the faithful might understand the religious basis. Also here is Record of the Restoration of the Chang'an House of Worship, by Li Qian, dated the sixth year of Jingtai (1455). Inside hang the Empress Dowager Cixi's plaque "Lineage Descended from the Kaaba" to the west and "The Ancient Teaching, Pure and True" to the east. The story behind them is specific: in the twenty-sixth year of Guangxu (1900), during the Boxer crisis, the Eight-Nation Alliance took Beijing and Cixi fled west to Xi'an with the Guangxu emperor; on their return the following year, in thanksgiving to the people and the gods, they bestowed inscribed plaques on Xi'an's mosques, temples, and monasteries, the official in charge being Tang Mianzhou, former acting provincial judge. The two given to this mosque were Cixi's "Lineage Descended from the Kaaba" and the emperor's "Teaching Revered in the Western Regions." At the court's center on the axis rises the Exhortation Tower (Xingshen Lou), the mosque's minaret or bangke lou — a three-story octagonal brick-and-timber structure with a pyramidal roof about 20 meters tall (another account gives two stories with triple eaves), its stages diminishing upward, wind-bells at the eaves, roofed in blue glazed tile. Its interior roof trusses are stacked timber members with no supporting columns — a rarity among Chinese pavilion structures. The name "Examine the Heart" comes from "I examine myself three times a day": deeply influenced by Neo-Confucianism, summoning the body is less than summoning the heart. Flanking it north and south are the imams' quarters, known colloquially as the "north and south official halls" for the constant comings and goings: the south hall, richly appointed, displays four calligraphic hanging scrolls inlaid in bamboo bark by Su Shi, Mi Fu, Yue Fei, and Huang Tingjian, and is fitted with lattice screens carrying the Eight Views of Chang'an; the north hall, the scripture hall, preserves a Ming manuscript Quran and a Qing-drawn Map of Mecca.

The gate to the fourth court is popularly called the "Three Linked Gates," its surfaces entirely carved in floral brickwork — a treasure of the genre. Three Arabic inscriptions run above: at center, "In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful"; to the south, "There is no god but God; Muhammad is the messenger of God"; to the north, "The mosque is the house of the reverent." At the court's center stands the Pavilion of the One Truth, also called the Phoenix Pavilion, combining a stele-tower with an open pavilion: a hexagonal pyramidal main pavilion flanked by two triangular wing pavilions, the three joined so that the whole resembles a phoenix spreading its wings. Within hangs the small dragon-carved plaque "The One Truth," written by Tie Xuan (also written Tie Xuan), Minister of War, in the first year of Jianwen (1399); facing east is a plaque "Embracing the Universe," drawn from the Quran. Two tile couplet boards flank it, bearing a thirty-character pair: "Ranked highest among sages, neither emperor nor king, yet ten thousand kingdoms joyfully submit to emperors and kings; his conduct set down in the true scripture, a way and a teaching, followed in the four quarters." Concisely, it encapsulates the life and virtues of the Prophet. To north and south stand seven-bay halls. The south hall was where the presiding imam received civil and military officials delivering imperial edicts; it displays Ming tables, chairs, porcelain, and calligraphy, and most precious of all, twelve boxwood longevity screens — four and a half meters tall, sixty centimeters wide, smooth as silk, each inlaid with a different design in precious materials, a gift of the Kangxi emperor; one screen carries an inlaid jade bee. Such a set of twelve is rare even in China. The north hall houses the Tang-era Stele Recording the Founding of the Mosque of the first year of Tianbao (742), the Jiajing-era Stele of the Restoration of the Mosque, and stone pieces including the "trial-official stone" and a sundial. Behind the pavilion lie two crab-apple-shaped fish ponds of clear water, each with a square pyramidal stele pavilion beside it. To the west rises a broad moon terrace of some 652 square meters, ringed by stone balustrade; three stone memorial archways stand along its western edge (one account places three stone gates along the east), a dragon-and-pearl carving set in the central imperial pathway, and, at its western end, the prayer hall. The broad terrace is also dotted with stone antiquities of successive dynasties that ornament the space: an ever-burning lamp of the Sui-Tang period, called the "Lucky Star Shining Aloft"; a white lotus pedestal of the Tang-Song era; a Ming "lotus rising in three tiers" pedestal; and an ancient stone basin, a "mouse stone," and a "snow slope stone." Here too stand a stone sundial and a "trial-official stone" studded thickly with iron nails. North of the hall rises a small timber pavilion, the Khutba Tower, where the prayer leader recites the invocation.

The prayer hall is the largest building in the mosque, first built in the twenty-fifth year of Hongwu (1392). Its plan is shaped like the character for "convex": seven bays across a width of 32.95 meters, nine bays across a depth of 38.53 meters, nearly 1,300 square meters in all, formed by two single-eave hip-gable roofs joined end to end, with a conical gilded finial and latticed carved doors between exposed columns. Being imperially commissioned, it is roofed in peacock-blue (one source says sky-blue) glazed tile; its roof ornaments were granted "by His Highness the Prince of Qin" through the Qin princely establishment's works office. It holds a thousand worshippers (another source says two thousand). The inner side walls flanking the front are faced with large brick carvings of flowers and fruit, finely worked and richly textured — among the finest Qing brick carving. Adjacent eave columns are doubled, carrying a north-south drainage channel above. The roof frame follows practices close to Song-dynasty palace halls: the king posts do not rise, bracket sets remain at the beam-column junctions, seemingly preserving the Ming condition; a ceiling is set below the frame; where the rear "tiger tail" meets the front, a large architrave is employed at the front rear eave to carry the frame, accommodating the mismatch between the tiger tail and the front columns. The outer-eave bracket sets are five-tier with double cantilevers. Most overwhelming is the coffered ceiling: over six hundred painted panels, each a medallion of Arabic script entwined with scrolling vines, centered on Quranic text in a seal-like form, varied in composition and color — a distinctive technique of Chinese Islamic temple painting. The hall is also lined with full-panel woodcut Quran text. Plaques accumulate in layers: below the front eave hangs the "Basmala," flanked by six restored plaques of differing shape and color, reading from right to left "Ever-Faithful to the One," "Universally Compassionate to All People," "Only the Purest and the One," "To Do Good Is the Greatest Joy," "Originally Pure and Exalted," and "The Way Crowned Through the Ages"; beneath the inner eave hangs "August Is the One Above," from the Daya section of the Book of Songs; in the second bay, "Having, Having, Having," expressing God's creative power and the re-creative power of the created — the middle "having" in standard script, the two flanking ones written in a single stroke, signifying that the "having" of the before and the after is continuous and unknown; in the fourth bay, "Reverently Following the August Heaven," likewise from the Daya. An eighty-character tile hanging couplet on the columns praises God's attributes through the words "unique" and "none." Behind the hall, three bays square, is the rear sanctuary, also roofed in peacock-blue tile; its west wall holds a concave niche, the mihrab, where the imam leads prayer and toward which the faithful turn. Islam forbids idolatry, so the space before it is entirely empty, serving only to mark direction; around the niche, walls are covered with full-floral designs and Kufic script. To the left of the niche stands the pulpit platform where the imam stands to preach; to the north, a small timber pavilion called the Khutba Tower, where the prayer leader recites the invocation. Moon gates at either side of the hall lead to a rear court containing the imams' graves.

The mosque as a whole is a Chinese Islamic complex built on the layout of traditional Chinese architecture while accommodating the requirements of a mosque. Its profundity lies in speaking Islamic doctrine through the grammar of Chinese building: the "one" of the central axis expresses the oneness of God; the north-south symmetry expresses the two paths of "knowing" and "practicing"; the plaques along the axis quote the Quran's descriptions of God, while those flanking it concern the Prophet's example, his station, and Chinese readings of Islam. And the worshipper entering from the east passes in sequence the warning "Heaven Is Watching Here," the self-scrutiny of "Examine the Heart," the purification of "In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful," until, at the moon terrace, the concrete reminder that "Children of Adam, dress properly for every prayer" — the sequence of architectural space is itself a design for leading the mind from the noise of the world into the state of prayer. In May 1997 it was named one of Xi'an's ten leading tourist sights.

米脂姜氏庄园

Jiang Family Manor, Mizhi

级别：国家级重点文物保护单位（第六批） 年代：清同治十三年（1874）动工，光绪十二年（1886）竣工 地址：榆林市米脂县城东约16公里桥河岔乡刘家峁村 类型：古民居·城堡式窑洞院落

从米脂县城沿佳米公路东南行十五六公里，朝东面一条沟里拐进，便到刘家峁村的牛家梁山脚。抬头仰望，一座城堡雄踞半山腰，寨墙矗立，古堡森严。若非刻意寻访而驱车疾行，几乎必会与之失之交臂——这正是陕北黄土高原特有的地理性格：它不像北京大栅栏的商宅、山西灵石的王家大院那样张扬，只是集中了乡野匠人的智慧，在平凡中现出奇崛。姜氏庄园又名姜耀祖宅院、姜氏庄院，由该村清末首富姜耀祖聘请北京专家设计，招聚县内能工巧匠兴建。它是黄土高原特有的窑洞院落与北方四合院相结合的民居形式，主体建筑为陕西地区最高等级的"明五暗四六厢窑"式窑洞院落，占地四十余亩（约二点六万至二点七万平方米），是全国最大的城堡式窑洞庄园，被专家称为"西部民居第一宅"。二〇〇六年五月二十五日由国务院公布为第六批全国重点文物保护单位。

姜家的发迹从姜耀祖的祖父姜安邦开始。姜安邦务农出身，早年在地主家揽工干活，因特别能吃苦赢得主家好感，主家将女儿许配给他。有了资本后他扩大经营，又放高利贷、收买土地，聚敛了大量财富；到姜耀祖时，姜家已拥有土地数十万亩（一说十八万余亩，范围扩及毗邻的绥德、子洲、佳县、吴堡、清涧等县），粮食数千石。姜安邦的第四子、姜耀祖的父亲姜锦塘五十四岁时开始修建宅院，未过几年便去世，年仅十九岁的姜耀祖继承父志，将宅院修造完成。工程于清同治十三年（1874）动土（另有同治十年、光绪十三年两说），光绪十二年（1886）竣工，前后耗时十三年（另有十六年一说）。据碑文记载："建筑自动工以至竣工约十五六年，宅分上中下三层，一切布置都由公独出心机，其规模之壮观，吾陕北所仅有。"当地老人回忆，修这宅院动土千万方，用石数万块，光小米就耗费九千余石，每年食盐还要用一石。

庄园依山势而建，由下院、中院、上院和寨墙、井楼等部分组成，三院暗道相通，四周寨墙高耸，对内相互通联，对外严于防患。进庄园先走甬道：一条从沟壑底部盘旋而上的石道，路面宽四米，中间以石片竖插排列，两侧分置宽一米的青石台阶供人行走——石片竖插既作为车马通道，又可在雨雪天防滑并排泄洪水。甬道中途有一个近乎九十度的直角转弯，这个拐弯并非随意：它使进攻者无法直冲寨门，也给守寨者留出了观察和射击的距离。甬道尽头是一间水房，内有井深三十三米的水井，水源引自山脚泉眼，至今活水不断，即使困守数月也无饮水之忧。井右侧有一个白石砌的巨型贮水石槽，做工精细，均以"一寸三錾"的斜錾和"一寸四錾"的立水錾打制而成；紧靠蓄水槽又是一个较小较浅的水槽，是洗衣之处。为了安装这个大石槽，当年由三十二个工匠从山脚下往高坡抬，抬到半山腰停搁难以挪动，是姜耀祖站在高坡上喊号，工人一齐用力才抬上井台。水房有两个铜钱镂空花饰的瓦窗，既夸示主人的雄厚实力，又可透过瓦窗清楚看到庄园外的一举一动，必要时还能通过窗眼向外射击。水房的地板装有排水装置；梁顶另有两根横梁，用来将主人抬到此处便不再入内的轿子吊起，防止发潮。寨墙最高处砌有炮台，形若马面，从井楼的窗口可直接射击攻打寨门者——一夫当关，万夫莫开。寨门为拱形石洞，极为窄仄，仅容两人并肩或一辆马车进入，门额镌主人亲笔题写的"大岳屏藩"四个大字，字体工整大方、苍劲有力，左首一行小字"姜耀祖建并书"，右首小字"光绪丙戊孟冬之吉"。这四字嵌了父子二人的名讳："岳"取自姜耀祖（字硕甫，又名姜海岳），"藩"取自长子姜树潘（字介屏），"大"为形容词，喻示先祖有大山一般的实力与气度，"福泽子孙，荫庇后人"，同时也有依恃高山抵御入侵者之意。

穿寨门过涵洞即达下院。下院院前以块石砌垒高达九点五米（一说九米多、约十米）的挡土墙，上部筑女儿墙，外观犹若城垣，首当庄园前冲。下院又称管家院，是陕北典型的窑洞四合院：正面三孔石窑，坐西北向东南，两厢各有三孔石窑，倒座是木屋架、石板铺顶的房屋。大门青瓦硬山顶，门额镶嵌"大夫第"行楷木雕金匾，气势夺人——姜耀祖捐过"从五品"奉直大夫，故称大夫第，这三字由清朝五大书法家之一的史年佑题写。门道两侧置抱鼓石。院墙上有一排带孔的石环即拴马石，共九个，是当年收缴租子时拴置骡马所用，可以想见人欢马叫的热闹场面。正面窑洞北侧（一说右侧）窑腿上开有一道小圆门，是通往中院和上院的阶梯暗道，过去也是佣人行走和小少爷们上下学的通道。下院既是佣人居所，也是办私塾的地方，又称书房院。院外东侧有石拱窑式井楼，高五米、东西宽四米、井深三十三米，井壁皆用石块盘旋垒砌而成，内安置手摇辘轳，不出寨门即可保证用水。

沿下院侧边道路穿洞门直插中院。正对中院门耸立着高八米、长约十米的寨墙（实为挡土墙），将庄园紧紧围绕，并留有通后山的门洞，上刻"保障"二字石刻匾额。中院坐东北向西南——与下院的坐西北向东南刻意扭转了朝向，是主人接待宾客和交际往来的场所。大门为五脊六兽硬山顶，门额悬挂"武魁"巨匾，以炫耀主人祖上（一说叔父）曾中武举之功绩。大门两侧各有石鼓门墩，上雕双狮捧面、麒麟负子、浮雕"福寿"图案。门内是水磨青砖影壁月亮门，内圆外方，曰"旭日东升"门，上有琴棋书画、姜太公钓鱼等人物，额饰砖雕"文王访贤"图，寓意主人情操高雅、怀才隐居、出将入相、大吉大利。院内方形石板铺地，宽敞明亮，倒座有石板铺顶的马棚，一排排拴马石扣井然有序，马槽雕琢之精细令当代石匠叹为观止——那时的马棚约等于今天的停车场，生意人的坐骑可不能随便停放。院中两侧各有三间大厢房，附小耳房：厢房两架梁，硬山顶，木隔扇门窗；耳房一架梁，卷棚顶，铺筒瓦。瓦筒卷棚，雕镂窗棂，精巧大方。**值得一提的是，东厢房比西厢房高二十厘米，这一差别源自中国古代宗法制度中的昭穆之制**。厢房两侧各有通道，东厢房北侧小过道连接葡萄院和东仓窑，并有石砌阶梯暗道连通上下院；西厢房北侧小过道连通碾磨院、西仓窑和应急外遁的窨子地道。东仓窑高五米、宽四点七米、深十四米，内有十二个大石仓和座于仓顶的小石仓，每仓储粮五十余石；东西仓窑合计可储粮六百余石（以每石二百公斤计，约合二十四万余公斤）。中院主要是账房和客人居住的场所。庄园的暗道纵深二百多米，可直达后山，洞内弯曲、多处设障，如遇特大危情可以藏身或脱险——只是岁月淤积、泥土松软，许多地方已经塌陷。

沿石级踏步而上，穿过垂花门即到第三层上院，这是整个建筑群的主宅，坐东北向西南，深十七点一米、宽十九点二米。正面一线五孔石窑，称上窑，每孔高四点五米、宽三点一米、进深八米，室内互通，正中三孔连通，均盘火炕，设暖阁、壁橱、水磨石灶，菱形青石板铺地，木门亮窗、双门双扇，檐头挑石举架、穿廊抱厦，上砌十字花墙。在五孔上窑的两侧分置对称的双院，院内面向西南各有两孔窑，俗称"暗四间"，门额上题刻"养廉""谦让"，是主人的厨院和库房。院子两侧各三孔厢窑，共六厢窑。上窑背后是与东西小院相通的两孔长约十四米（一说深四丈二尺）的大"枕头窑"，作贮粮仓库，内砌大石仓十二个。这套格局即陕北最高等级的"明五暗四六厢窑"：五孔正窑涵义"五子登科"，主人夫妇居中，侧配分居两旁各室；"暗四"指上窑两旁的套院，涵义"四喜盈门"（一说"四喜发财"）；"六厢窑"指两侧六孔窑洞，涵义"六六大顺"，供晚辈居住。三者合数为十五，无论分合均取单数——单数为增，双数为圆，希冀人口增加、财产增值，隐含"人财两旺"之意。院中有一张大石床，是主人夏日乘凉、夜夜休息之处；环绕石床底部有一道石槽，晚上倒入水即可隔离虫蝎侵袭，乡人称之为庄园的"环城河"。当年主人还在院中亲手栽植两棵老槐树，枝繁叶茂。

上院的垂花门是整座宅院的精品，也是全宅砖、木、石三雕艺术的集中体现。门楼为砖木结构，柱梁门框举架，双瓣驼峰托枋，小爪状雀替，额枋、荷墩彩绘，"富贵"雀替，"牡丹""夏莲"雕空花板，莲花牡丹花板，木构件皆彩绘，卷棚顶，四柱双层门，青瓦卷棚。绿色门扇镶铜制铺首衔环、流云剔角、黄铜云钩与踢角，三十个五路泡钉依次排列——既对门心板起牢固连接作用，又在外观上表现出不可侵犯的威严。门蹲处置方形石雕门跪，上卧浮雕"狮子滚绣球"，侧雕鹤、鹿、松、竹、夏莲、牡丹，争芳斗妍。门楼两侧各设神龛与护墙，敬祀天地神位。照壁为水磨砖雕廊心画，画面是"鹿奔松林，鹤唳寿石，日月相映"——以"鹿鹤"谐音寄意于东、西、南、北、上、下"六合"，合称"鹿鹤同春"，寓意国泰民安、社会稳定、日月生辉、福寿无疆。与大门连为一体的屏风门，以圆鼓形基石柱木顶立，整座门楼威严气派。庄园的窗棂图案更是数不胜数，有"枪头梅花格""三交嵌牡丹""如意格""卍字格""双龙戏珠""盘长"等。综观全园，无处不雕、无处不琢：大至整个建筑设计，小到各个微小装置，一楼一窗一棱一匾都在阐述一个哲理，或抒发一种心情，或祝愿金榜题名，或颂扬一种教义，可谓"求工于一笔之内，寄情于点画之间"，"装饰与教化相融，艺术与精神共存"。

姜耀祖去世后，姜家后人在此安然居住数十年。民国三十六年（1947）实行土地制度改革，庄园除留上院正窑仍归姜家后人外，其余部分均分给贫雇农；农业合作社时期，姜家作为当地最大的地主，宅子被划分给贫农，至今仍有几户人家居住，其中姜耀祖的四世孙姜纯哲一直住在庄园角上的窑洞里，用心维护着祖业。一九九八年，姜氏庄园作为汉窑洞建筑的典范，以一比一的比例仿建于北京中华民族园。二〇〇一年六月十日，中央电视台《东方时空·直播时刻》栏目对姜氏庄园作现场直播，引起中外专家学者关注；电视剧《血色浪漫》中钟跃民上山下乡的一段，也在此拍摄。姜氏庄园与山西平遥乔家大院、河南巩义康百万庄园等并称为中国最美的十大民居建筑之一。二〇〇〇年由米脂县人民政府公布为县级重点文物保护单位，二〇〇一年十二月十二日成立姜氏民宅文物管理所，二〇〇三年九月二十四日由陕西省人民政府公布为第四批省级重点文物保护单位，二〇〇六年五月二十五日由国务院公布为第六批全国重点文物保护单位。此后，二〇〇四年开展移民搬迁，二〇〇五年政府正式接管并对外开放，二〇〇八年实施一期抢救性维修和停车场硬化，二〇一三年开工改造维修，二〇一四年实施寨墙抢救性维修工程。

Some fifteen or sixteen kilometres southeast of Mizhi county town along the Jia-Mi highway, a turn east into a gully brings one to the foot of Niujialiang Hill in Liujiamao village. Looking up, a castle dominates the hillside, its ramparts standing stark against the sky — a place one would surely miss passing at speed, so unlike the merchant houses of Beijing's Dashilan or the Wang family compound of Lingshi, Shanxi. This reserve is characteristic of the loess plateau: it concentrates the ingenuity of rural craftsmen and reveals its strangeness and grandeur within the ordinary. The Jiang Family Manor, also called the Jiang Yaozu Residence or the Jiang Compound, was built by Jiang Yaozu, the wealthiest man in the village in the late Qing, who engaged experts from Beijing to design it and gathered skilled craftsmen from the county to build it. It combines the cave-dwelling courtyard peculiar to the loess plateau with the northern Chinese siheyuan; its principal structure is the highest-grade "five open, four concealed, six flanking caves" (ming wu an si liu xiang yao) courtyard of the Shaanxi region. Covering some forty mu (roughly 26,000–27,000 square meters), it is the largest castle-style cave-dwelling manor in China and has been called by specialists "the foremost residence of western Chinese vernacular architecture." On 25 May 2006 the State Council proclaimed it a National Key Protected Heritage Site in the sixth batch.

The Jiang family's rise began with Jiang Yaozu's grandfather, Jiang Anbang. A farmer by origin, he hired himself out to a landlord, and his exceptional hard work won the landlord's favour and his daughter's hand. With capital in hand he expanded his operations, lent money at interest, and bought land, amassing great wealth; by Jiang Yaozu's time the family held hundreds of thousands of mu of land (one account gives over 180,000 mu, extending into the neighbouring counties of Suide, Zizhou, Jia, Wubu, and Qingjian) and thousands of shi of grain. Jiang Anbang's fourth son, Jiang Jintang, Jiang Yaozu's father, began building at fifty-four but died a few years later; Jiang Yaozu, then just nineteen, inherited his father's ambition and completed the work. Construction began in the thirteenth year of Tongzhi (1874) — other accounts give the tenth year of Tongzhi or the thirteenth year of Guangxu — and was completed in the twelfth year of Guangxu (1886), taking thirteen years (another account says sixteen). A stele records: "From groundbreaking to completion took some fifteen or sixteen years; the residence is divided into upper, middle, and lower levels, and every arrangement was devised by the master alone. The grandeur of its scale is unmatched in northern Shaanxi." Local elders recall that the building moved a thousand thousand cubic yards of earth and used tens of thousands of stones; millet alone consumed over nine thousand shi, with a further shi of salt each year.

The manor climbs the slope in three courts — lower, middle, and upper — with defensive walls and a well-tower, the three linked by hidden passages, ramparts rising all around: internally connected, externally sealed against threat. One approaches by a stone causeway winding up from the floor of the ravine, four meters wide, its center paved with stone slips set on edge and flanked by one-meter bluestone steps for pedestrians. The slips laid on edge served both as a carriageway and, in rain or snow, as a non-slip surface that drained the water. Midway the causeway turns at almost a right angle — no arbitrary choice: the bend prevents attackers from charging the gate directly and gives defenders distance from which to observe and shoot. At the top stands a water house containing a well thirty-three meters deep, fed from a spring at the foot of the hill and still flowing today, so that even months under siege would bring no fear of thirst. To the right of the well is a huge stone cistern of white stone, finely worked, every surface cut with the "three-chisel-per-inch" slanting and "four-chisel-per-inch" vertical tooling. Beside it sits a shallower trough for washing. To install the great cistern, thirty-two workmen had to carry it up the slope from the foot of the hill; when they stalled halfway, Jiang Yaozu stood above and called the count, and with a single concerted heave they brought it onto the well platform. The water house has two windows of openwork tile in a coin pattern — at once a display of the owner's wealth, a vantage from which all movement outside could be watched, and, if need be, loopholes through which to fire. The floor has a drainage installation; two beams in the roof served to hoist the master's sedan chair — carried this far and no further — clear of the damp. At the highest point of the rampart a gun platform is built in the form of a mamian bastion, from whose window anyone attacking the gate could be shot directly: one man holding the pass, ten thousand unable to break through. The gate itself is an arched stone tunnel, exceedingly narrow — barely wide enough for two abreast or a single cart — its lintel carved with four characters in the owner's own hand: "Da Yue Ping Fan." To the left runs a small inscription, "Built and written by Jiang Yaozu"; to the right, "An auspicious early winter, bingxu year of Guangxu." The four characters embed the names of father and son: yue from Jiang Yaozu (courtesy name Shuofu, also called Jiang Haiyue), fan from his eldest son Jiang Shupan (courtesy name Jieping); da is an adjective. Together they declare that the ancestor had the strength and bearing of a great mountain, that his blessing would "shelter his descendants and shade those who come after," and at the same time that the manor relies on the high hill to repel invaders.

Passing through the gate and its tunnel brings one to the lower court. In front of it a revetment of cut stone rises nine and a half meters (other accounts say over nine, or about ten), topped by a parapet, so that from without it reads as a city wall and takes the first shock of any assault. The lower court, also called the steward's court, is a typical northern Shaanxi cave-dwelling quadrangle: three stone caves at the front facing northwest to southeast, three more on each side, and at the back a timber-framed house roofed in stone slabs. The gate has a flush-gable roof of grey tiles and carries a gold-inlaid plaque in running-standard script, "Dafudi" — "House of the Grand Master" — impressive in its presence. Jiang Yaozu had purchased the rank of a fifth-grade junior "fengzhi dafu," hence the title; the three characters were written by Shi Nianyou, one of the five great calligraphers of the Qing. Drum-shaped stones flank the gate passage. A row of perforated stone rings set in the courtyard wall — nine in all — are tethering stones, where mules and horses were tied when rents were collected; one can imagine the bustle. In the north pier of the front caves (one source says the right) a small round door opens onto a stepped secret passage to the middle and upper courts, once used by servants and by the young masters going to and from school. The lower court housed servants and also ran a private school, hence its other name, Study Court. Outside the court to the east stands a stone-arch well-tower, five meters high and four meters wide, its shaft thirty-three meters deep, walls spirally laid in cut stone, fitted with a hand-cranked windlass so that water was available without leaving the gate.

A path along the side of the lower court leads through a tunnel gate directly into the middle court. Opposite its entrance rises an eight-meter, ten-meter-long defensive wall — in effect a revetment — enclosing the manor and pierced by a gate opening onto the rear hill, inscribed with two characters, "Baozhang" ("Safeguard"). The middle court faces northeast to southwest, its orientation deliberately twisted away from the lower court's northwest-to-southeast; this was where the master received guests and conducted social business. The gate has a flush-gable roof with five ridges and six beasts, and carries a great plaque, "Wukui" — "Military Champion" — celebrating an ancestor's (or, in one account, an uncle's) success in the military examinations. Stone drum bases flank the gate, carved with two lions presenting faces, a qilin carrying its young, and "fortune and longevity" reliefs. Within is a polished grey-brick screen wall with a moon gate, round within and square without, called the "Rising Sun" gate; it bears figures representing the four arts — zither, chess, calligraphy, painting — and Jiang Taigong fishing, with a brick carving of "King Wen Visiting the Sage" above the lintel, signifying the owner's refined taste, his worth concealed in retirement, his readiness to serve as general or minister, and his hopes of great good fortune. The court is paved with square stone flags, spacious and bright; at the back stands a stable roofed in stone slabs, its rows of tethering rings neatly ordered and its manger carved with a finesse that astonishes modern stonemasons — the stable of that era was the equivalent of today's car park, and a merchant's mount could not simply be left anywhere. Three large side rooms with small wing rooms stand on each side, with round-tile barrel roofs and carved lattice windows, at once refined and generous. Notably, the east side rooms stand twenty centimeters higher than the west — a difference arising from the zhaomu ordering of the ancient Chinese kinship system. Passages flank the rooms: north of the east rooms a small corridor leads to the grape court and east granary caves, with a stone stair and hidden passage connecting the upper and lower courts; north of the west rooms another corridor reaches the milling court, the west granary, and a tunnel (yinzi) for emergency escape. The east granary cave is five meters high, 4.7 meters wide, and fourteen meters deep, holding twelve large stone bins plus smaller bins set atop them, each holding over fifty shi. Together the east and west granaries could store some six hundred shi — at two hundred kilograms the shi, roughly 240,000 kilograms. The middle court served chiefly as the accounts office and guest quarters. The manor's hidden passages run over two hundred meters to the rear hill, winding and repeatedly obstructed, offering refuge or escape in extreme danger — though silt and soft earth have collapsed much of their length.

Stone steps lead up through a festooned gate to the third and highest level, the upper court, the principal residence of the whole complex. Facing northeast to southwest, it measures 17.10 meters deep and 19.2 meters wide. Along the front run five stone caves, the "upper caves," each 4.5 meters high, 3.1 meters wide, and eight meters deep, interconnected within; the central three open into one another and are fitted with heated kang, warm cupboards, wall cabinets, and polished stone stoves, floored in diamond-shaped bluestone slabs, with latticed wooden doors of double leaf and bright windows. The eaves are carried on projecting stone corbels with a veranda, above which runs a lattice-screen wall. Symmetrically placed on either side of the five caves are two small courts, each holding two caves facing southwest — the "four concealed" rooms, whose lintels are inscribed "Cultivate Integrity" and "Modesty and Yield"; these were the kitchen court and storerooms. Three flanking caves on each side of the yard make the "six flanking caves." Behind the upper caves run two great "pillow caves" some fourteen meters long (another account gives four zhang two chi), connected to the small east and west courts and serving as granaries, each fitted with twelve large stone bins. This arrangement is the highest grade of northern Shaanxi dwelling, the "five open, four concealed, six flanking caves": the five front caves signify "five sons succeeding in the examinations," with the master and his wife at the center and junior branches flanking; the "four concealed" signify "four joys fill the gate" (another account reads "four joys bring wealth"); the "six flanking caves" signify "all smoothness six times over" and house the younger generation. The three numbers total fifteen — odd whether counted together or apart; odd numbers signify increase, even numbers completion, expressing the hope that both family and fortune would grow, that "people and wealth both flourish." In the courtyard stands a large stone bed where the master rested in summer heat and at night; a channel running around its base could be filled with water to keep insects and scorpions away — locals call it the manor's "moat." The master himself planted two old locust trees in the court, which grew thick with foliage.

The festooned gate of the upper court is the finest element of the manor and the place where its brick, wood, and stone carving converge. The gatehouse is brick and timber, with posts, beams, and door frame raised on a truss, double-lobed camel-hump braces carrying the architrave, painted architraves and bearing blocks, "wealth and honor" brackets, and openwork panels of "peony" and "summer lotus" as well as lotus-and-peony boards; every timber member is painted, beneath a round-ridge roof of grey tiles with four posts and a double-tier door. The green door leaves are set with bronze door-knocker rings, cloud hooks, and angled studs, with thirty studs arranged in five rows — both fastening the panels firmly and projecting an inviolable severity. At the door base sit square carved stone kneelers, on which crouch lions in relief "rolling a brocaded ball," their sides carved with cranes, deer, pine, bamboo, summer lotus, and peony in competing bloom. Niches and screen walls flank the gatehouse for the worship of heaven and earth. The screen wall carries a polished brick wall-painting: "deer running through pine woods, cranes calling at longevity rocks, sun and moon shining upon one another" — the homophones lu (deer) and he (crane) standing for the six directions east, west, south, north, above, below, the whole known as "Deer and Crane in Spring," signifying national peace, social stability, the radiance of sun and moon, and boundless fortune and longevity. The screen door joined to the gate rests on drum-shaped stone footings with timber posts, giving the whole gatehouse an air of command. The window-lattice patterns throughout are innumerable: "spearhead and plum blossom," "three-intersecting inlaid peony," "ruyi," "swastika," "twin dragons playing with a pearl," "endless knot," and more. Throughout the manor there is nowhere uncarved, nowhere unworked: from the design of the whole down to the smallest fitting, every bay, window, mullion, and plaque sets forth a principle, voices a sentiment, wishes success in the examinations, or praises a teaching — "craft sought within a single stroke, feeling lodged between dot and line," where "ornament and moral instruction merge, and art and spirit coexist."

After Jiang Yaozu's death his descendants lived here in peace for several decades. In the thirty-sixth year of the Republic (1947) land reform was carried out: apart from the upper court's main caves, which remained with the Jiang heirs, the rest was distributed to poor and landless peasants. In the agricultural cooperative period, as the largest local landlord family, the Jiangs' house was allotted to poor peasants, and several households still live in it; among them Jiang Chunshe, a fourth-generation descendant of Jiang Yaozu, has continued to live in a cave at the compound's corner and to care for his ancestral home. In 1998 the manor was reproduced at 1:1 scale in Beijing's Chinese Ethnic Culture Park as an exemplar of Han cave-dwelling architecture. On 10 June 2001 CCTV's "Oriental Horizon · Live Moment" broadcast live from the manor, drawing the attention of specialists and scholars in China and abroad; scenes of Rustic Romance, a television drama in which the character Zhong Yuemin is sent to the countryside, were also filmed here. The Jiang Family Manor is counted among China's ten most beautiful vernacular buildings, alongside the Qiao family compound of Pingyao in Shanxi and the Kang Baiwan manor of Gongyi in Henan. It was listed as a county-level protected site by the Mizhi county government in 2000; a management office was established on 12 December 2001; Shaanxi Province listed it as a provincial protected site in the fourth batch on 24 September 2003; and on 25 May 2006 the State Council named it a National Key Protected Heritage Site in the sixth batch. Since then: relocation of residents in 2004, government takeover and public opening in 2005, a first-phase rescue restoration and parking-area paving in 2008, a reconstruction and repair project begun in 2013, and emergency works on the defensive walls in 2014.

西安古城墙

Xi'an City Wall

级别：国家级重点文物保护单位（第一批） 年代：明洪武七年至十一年（1374—1378）建成，历代修葺 地址：西安市中心区 类型：古城墙·夯土包砖城防建筑

西安城墙是中国现存规模最大、保存最完整的古代城垣，也是一九六一年三月四日国务院公布的第一批全国重点文物保护单位中唯一的城垣建筑。广义的西安城墙包括唐城墙与明城墙，一般特指狭义上的明城墙。它建于明洪武七年至十一年（1374—1378），系在唐长安城皇城和元代奉元城基础上修筑而成——从隋唐皇城算起，这段城垣已有一千四百多年历史，从明初扩建府城算起也有六百多年。城墙平面呈封闭的长方形，东西长四千二百五十六米、南北宽二千七百零八米，周长十三点七四公里（另有十三点七二三八四公里、十三点七九公里、十一点九公里诸说；若按东墙二五九〇米、西墙二六三一点二米、南墙三四四一点六米、北墙三二四一米相加，则为一一九〇三点八米，差别当因是否计入瓮城等附属部分），墙高十二米，底宽十五至十八米，顶宽十二至十五米——厚度大于高度，稳固如山，墙顶可以跑车和操练。城墙内人们习称古城区，面积十一点三二平方公里，钟鼓楼即位于古城区中心。

这段城垣的历史是层层叠压的。隋开皇二年（582）建大兴城；唐武德元年（618）改称长安城，高宗永徽五年（654）初建唐城墙。唐末天祐元年（904），驻防长安的佑国军节度使韩建认为原来城大不易防守，放弃了外郭城与宫城，只把皇城加以改修，封闭了朱雀、安福、延喜三门，北开玄武门以便防守，改筑后的长安城称作"新城"。明洪武二年（1369）大将军徐达率军渡河入陕，元守将遁逃，明朝廷改奉元路为西安府——"西安"之名由此而始。洪武七年至十一年（1374—1378）正式修筑城墙，用黄土分层夯打，最底层用土、石灰和糯米汁混合夯打，异常坚硬；每层黄土厚约八至十二厘米，夯打紧密，墙基由马兰黄土加三分之一石灰组成，下方是厚达一点六米的三合土墙基，并加少许石灰、细沙、麦秸节和水调和。初建时为夯土墙，四面各开一门：东长乐门，西安定门，南永宁门，北安远门。明嘉靖五年（1526）重修城楼。明隆庆二年（1568），陕西巡抚张祉主持修复，将城墙外壁和顶面砌了青砖，使土城墙第一次变成砖城墙。崇祯九年（1636），陕西巡抚孙传庭为防农民起义军，又修筑四关土城墙（即四关郭城），今四梢门即四关郭门位置。清代曾对城墙进行过十二次补修，其中以乾隆四十六年至五十一年（1781—1786）陕西巡抚毕沅主持的工程最大——他给整个城墙外壁加厚了砖面，疏浚护城河，增设排水道、垛口和女儿墙，城墙墙体自此基本定型，与城河、内环路形成呼应配合的城防体系。

墙体构造极有讲究。外层包砌青砖，城砖靠基石处厚二米、顶部厚一米；明代砖一般长三十八厘米、宽十八厘米、厚五厘米，清代砖略大，长四十五厘米、宽二十三厘米、厚十厘米——清代砖砌于城表，明代砖包于内层，这也是判断城墙修筑年代的直接依据。城顶为"海墁"，即城墙顶部的平面：先添夯三合土（一说厚二十厘米，一说厚零点四五米），上面再平铺两至三层面砖（一说二层城砖，一说二三层面砖即二三层面砖铺成海墁），并统一全城海墁内倾斜坡约五度，以便雨水较快地导入城墙内侧的流水槽。城墙内壁每隔四十至六十米用青砖砌筑一道溜水槽和吐水嘴，形成完善的排水系统。墙顶外沿筑垛墙，高约二米，共五千九百八十四个垛口，可射箭、瞭望，又能有效躲避敌人箭矢；内沿建半人高的女儿墙，高零点八五米，无垛口，作用是防止士兵及辎重往来时跌落。城墙外侧每隔一百二十米设一座突出墙外的敌台（亦称马面、墩台），全城共九十八座，各宽二十米、向外伸出十一米（一说十二米）。一百二十米的间距并非任意——它恰好是古代弓弩有效射程的一半，使相邻两座敌台能够形成交叉火力，射杀攀城之敌。城四隅各有一座突出城墙的角台，上建角楼；除西南角台保持唐代（一说元代）建制为圆形外，其余均为方形——这个圆角是唐皇城转角原状留下的孤证。西南角今尚存八角形角楼的遗址。城墙外围护城河环绕，河宽十八米、深六米、长十四点六公里（另有宽三十余米、深十二至十五米之说），与城墙、城门组成三位一体的城防建筑体系。

四座城门各有门楼三重，由外而内为闸楼、箭楼、正楼，各楼间均有城墙相连，构成"重城三楼"的防卫体系：箭楼与正楼之间为瓮城，与闸楼之间为月城。闸楼在最外，其作用是升降吊桥，兼打更报时、有敌情时发警报；箭楼居中，是瓮城的主体防御设施；正楼（俗称城楼）在最里，楼下即城的正门。四门瓮城形制各异：东门和西门瓮城各有四门，南门瓮城有三门（无南门，故永宁门是唯一没有箭楼的城门），北门瓮城有南、北二门。东、西瓮城在正楼、箭楼下各开一高宽约六米的券门，又在瓮城南、北两侧各开一门。正楼尚存东、西、南三座，形制基本相同，为二层重楼：以西门正楼为例，通面阔七间四十三点二米，进深二间十九点二米，高三十六米（另有三十二米、三十三米之说）；周围廊，重檐三滴水歇山顶；通檐用七架梁，前后带单步梁；檐下及平座均施斗栱，底层与平座斗栱均为三踩单下昂，顶层斗栱为五踩双下昂（一说五踩双下昂或单翘单下昂，后尾鎏金斗栱，平座斗栱减一铺为三踩单翘）；楼壁厚约二米，内设木楼板、楼梯供上下使用，下部辟砖券门洞。箭楼尚存东、西、北三座，矗立于瓮城外端墙正中，以青砖为主结构：以西门箭楼为例，通面阔十一间五十三米，进深二间十六点三米，高三十三点四米；外侧单檐歇山顶，内侧三滴水带檐廊，屋面覆青灰色筒板瓦。其构架为檐柱直接承托梁架，中柱则直接通至三架梁下而不直抵脊檩；中柱前后使用短梁（穿梁）与檐柱相交，仅明间两缝施通檐七架梁。楼身内架四层，上下置木楼梯，供守城士兵登临箭窗；正面分设四层箭窗，每层十二孔（明间两孔），共四十八孔；两侧面各三层，每层三孔，共十八孔——合计六十六孔。楼三面包砌砖壁。正楼和箭楼梁架均为彻上明造，歇山转角做法：有的为抹角梁与顺、扒梁并用，有的仅用顺梁而无抹角梁，可能因不同时期修葺所造成。

城墙高于地面，须有上下城的通道。明代西安城共建有十一处登城马道，其中七处建于城墙周边内侧，四处建于四城门内城楼左侧（另一说沿城墙曾设六处、现存三处）。建于内侧的七处皆为砖砌滚坡式建筑，坡道右边内侧有一米五宽、便于步兵上下的阶梯道；马道坡呈二十五度，宽六米，高与城顶海墁齐，贴于城墙内壁，为双阶梯左右两坡、形状呈等腰梯形，底边长一百米、顶边长十米。马道旁砌有护墙，下端道口设门栅，大门朱漆，称"大红门"，驻兵防守。四门城楼内左侧各有一处登城马道，沿城墙另有六处，马道长约一百米、宽约六米。瓮城中还有通向城头的马道，缓上无台阶，便于战马上下。

四座主城门各有来历。永宁门是正南门，也是西安城门中资格最老、沿用时间最长的一座，始建于隋初开皇二年（582），原是皇城南面三座门中偏东的安上门，唐末韩建缩建新城时留作南门，明代改称永宁门。安定门为正西门，本是唐皇城西面中门顺义门，唐末缩建时保留下来，明代扩建城墙时位置略向南移并取今名，寓意西部边疆安泰康定；它是我国迄今为止保存最完整的古城堡，文物专家在其建于明初的城内发现了保存六百余年、面积约一千余平方米的彩绘，为研究明代彩绘工艺与建筑艺术史提供了重要资料——彩绘之所以能大面积保存，是因为正楼用途比较单纯，主要是军事指挥部，即明代西北王的"司令部"，与箭楼的实战用途不同。安远门为北门，唐代时便是迎宾的主要大门，被称为"天下第一门"，"安远"二字取继承中原王朝对边远少数民族的怀柔安抚之意；辛亥革命（1911）时城楼被焚毁。长乐门为东门，取祈祝大明江山长久欢乐之意；明崇祯十六年（1643），长乐门建成未满二百七十年时，被李自成起义军付之一炬，清代重建；门楣"长乐门"三字是民国元年（1912）六月陕西督军张氏所题；西安事变前，张学良将军曾在东门城楼上组建教导队和学兵队，这一旧址被修复作为西安事变纪念地。此外，南门正楼在辛亥革命时毁于炮火，南门箭楼于一九二六年焚于火，四门闸楼及月城均在清末至民国初年被拆除，北门正楼和南门箭楼分别于一九一一年和一九二六年焚毁。今尚存正楼、箭楼六座：南门正楼，西门正楼、箭楼，北门箭楼，东门正楼、箭楼。

从民国开始，为方便出入古城区，先后新辟了中山门（1927）、玉祥门、解放门（1934，原名中正门）、勿幕门（1939）、建国门、和平门（1953）、文昌门（1986）、朱雀门（1986）、含光门、朝阳门（1994）等，现城墙已有城门十八座（一说连同四座主城门共十八座，另有十六处券门豁口之说）。其中值得一提的是，一九八四年整修城墙时发掘出含光门遗址，发现花岗石制作的柱础与刻花的门槛门道，景区将新建券洞城门置于遗址东侧，对遗址作框架结构保护、外包城砖，使外观与城墙一致，内设人工采光和空调系统，建成唐皇城墙含光门遗址博物馆；一九八五年又发掘出包裹在明城墙内的朱雀门遗址，一九八六年重新开启。城墙上还有一处与军事防御无关的设施——魁星楼，明清时西安府学和孔庙（今碑林博物馆）建在城墙旁边，魁星楼即建在城墙上，在历史风云中屡遭破坏，一九八六年得以修复，其下新开的城门被命名为文昌门。早年及近年整修过程中，于西墙和南墙内均发现有唐代城墙和城门遗迹。一九八三年至一九九一年间，财政部、陕西省和西安市人民政府共投资二亿多元人民币对城墙进行全面整修，除补葺墙砖、维修城门楼和四角墩台外，还复原魁星楼一座、敌楼十座，并沿护城河建起环城公园。一九九一年于南门瓮城外重修月城、闸楼及吊桥，二〇一三年重建南门箭楼。解放门原名中正门，一九三四年因陇海铁路通车西安而修，一九五二年因扩建火车站广场而拆除城墙，豁口通道宽五百一十六米；二〇〇五年重新连接，下开三个状似拱桥的城门，跨度合计二百米，其中中间城门跨度达八十四米、两侧各五十八米——自此西安城墙全线贯通。

The Xi'an city wall is the largest and best-preserved surviving ancient city wall in China, and the only fortification structure among the first batch of National Key Protected Heritage Sites proclaimed by the State Council on 4 March 1961. Broadly, "Xi'an city wall" includes both the Tang and Ming walls, but the term usually refers specifically to the Ming wall. It was built between the seventh and eleventh years of Hongwu (1374–1378) on the foundations of the Tang imperial city and the Yuan-era Fengyuan city — counting from the Sui-Tang imperial city, the line of fortification has a history of over 1,400 years; counting from the Ming expansion of the prefectural city, more than 600. Its plan is a closed rectangle — 4,256 meters east to west and 2,708 meters north to south — with a perimeter of 13.74 kilometres (other figures given are 13.72384 km, 13.79 km, and 11.9 km; adding the east wall at 2,590 m, west 2,631.2 m, south 3,441.6 m, and north 3,241 m gives 11,903.8 m, the discrepancy presumably depending on whether barbicans and appended works are counted). The wall is 12 meters high, 15–18 meters wide at the base, and 12–15 meters wide at the top — its thickness exceeding its height, stable as a mountain, its crown broad enough for carts and drill. The area within, conventionally called the old city, covers 11.32 square kilometres, with the Bell and Drum Towers at its center.

The history of this fortification is layered. In the second year of Kaihuang, Sui (582), Daxingcheng was built; in the first year of Wude, Tang (618) it was renamed Chang'an, and in the fifth year of Yonghui (654) the Tang wall was first constructed. In the first year of Tianyou, late Tang (904), Han Jian, military commissioner of Youguo Army defending Chang'an, judged the existing city too large to defend, abandoned the outer city and palace city, and refitted only the imperial city, sealing its Zhuque, Anfu, and Yanxi gates and opening a Xuanwu gate to the north for defensive convenience; the rebuilt city was called the "new city." In the second year of Hongwu (1369) the general Xu Da crossed the river from Shanxi into Shaanxi; the Yuan garrison fled, and the Ming court renamed Fengyuan Circuit as Xi'an Prefecture — the origin of the name Xi'an. Between the seventh and eleventh years of Hongwu (1374–1378) the wall was formally built, of rammed loess in layers, the lowest courses of earth mixed with lime and glutinous rice paste, exceptionally hard; each layer of loess is about 8–12 cm thick and tightly tamped, the footing composed of Malan loess with one third lime over a 1.6-meter-thick layer of lime-soil base, tempered with a little lime, fine sand, and chopped wheat straw. It began as a rammed-earth wall with one gate per side: Changle (east), Anding (west), Yongning (south), and Anyuan (north). Gate towers were rebuilt in the fifth year of Jiajing (1526). In the second year of Longqing (1568), Zhang Zhi, governor of Shaanxi, directed repairs that faced the outer wall and crown with grey brick, turning the earthen wall into a brick wall for the first time. In the ninth year of Chongzhen (1636), Sun Chuanting, governor of Shaanxi, built earthen walls for the four suburbs — the four guo — against the peasant rebel armies; today's four "shao men" mark the positions of those suburban gates. The Qing carried out twelve rounds of repair, the largest directed by Bi Yuan, governor of Shaanxi, between the forty-sixth and fifty-first years of Qianlong (1781–1786): he thickened the brick facing of the whole outer wall, dredged the moat, and added drainage channels, crenellations, and parapets. The wall's fabric was essentially fixed at this point, forming a coordinated defensive system with the moat and the inner ring road.

The construction of the wall body is highly considered. The outer facing is grey brick, two meters thick at the footing and one meter at the top. Ming brick is generally 38 cm long, 18 cm wide, and 5 cm thick; Qing brick is somewhat larger at 45 × 23 × 10 cm — the Qing brick laid on the surface, the Ming brick within, which serves as direct evidence for dating the fabric. The crown, called haiman, was built up with a layer of lime-soil (given variously as 20 centimeters and 0.45 meters), then paved with two or three courses of brick, the whole given a consistent inward slope of about five degrees so that rainwater drains quickly to the channels on the inner face. Every 40–60 meters along the inner face, a brick chute and spout carry water off, forming a complete drainage system. Along the outer edge runs a crenellated parapet about two meters high with 5,984 embrasures, for shooting, observation, and cover; along the inner edge a waist-high parapet 0.85 meters high without embrasures, to prevent soldiers and supplies from falling. Every 120 meters along the outer face projects a defensive platform (also called mamian or dun tai), ninety-eight in all, each 20 meters wide and projecting 11 meters (one source says 12). The 120-meter interval is not arbitrary — it is half the effective range of ancient crossbows, so that adjacent platforms could deliver crossing fire against attackers scaling the wall. At each corner a projecting corner platform carried a corner tower; all are square except the southwest, which is round, preserving Tang (or, by another account, Yuan) practice — that round corner is the sole surviving evidence of the Tang imperial city's original turn. The remnants of an octagonal corner tower survive at the southwest today. Outside, the moat encircles the wall: 18 meters wide, 6 meters deep, and 14.6 kilometres long (another account gives over 30 meters wide and 12–15 meters deep), forming with wall and gates a three-part defensive system.

Each of the four gates has three towers: from outside inward, the sluice tower (zha lou), the arrow tower (jian lou), and the main tower (zheng lou), linked by stretches of wall in the "triple-tower, double-wall" defensive arrangement. The space between arrow tower and main tower is the barbican (weng cheng); between arrow tower and sluice tower, the crescent enclosure (yue cheng). The sluice tower, outermost, raised and lowered the drawbridge and also kept the night watches and sounded alarms; the arrow tower at the center is the barbican's principal defensive work; the main tower, innermost, stands over the true gate. The four barbicans differ: those of the east and west gates have four openings each; the south, three (with no south gate, whence Yongning's lack of an arrow tower); the north, two, to south and north. At east and west, arched openings about six meters high and wide pierce the wall beneath main and arrow towers, with a further gate on each flank. Three main towers survive, at east, west, and south, all essentially of one form: two-story double structures. Taking the west gate's as the example, it measures seven bays across a width of 43.2 meters, two bays across a depth of 19.2 meters, and 36 meters high (other figures: 32 and 33 meters); it has a surrounding veranda and a double-eave, triple-tier hip-gable roof; seven-rafter beams spanning the full depth with single-step beams fore and aft; bracket sets at the eaves and at the balustrade level, three-tier with single cantilever below and at the balcony, five-tier with double cantilevers at the top (another account: five-tier double cantilever or single-rising single-cantilever, with gilded bracket tails, the balcony brackets reduced by one tier to three-tier single-rising). Its walls are about two meters thick, with timber floors and stairs inside, and a brick-arched passage at the base. Three arrow towers survive, at east, west, and north, standing at the center of the barbican's outer wall and built chiefly of grey brick. The west gate's measures eleven bays across 53 meters, two bays across a depth of 16.3 meters, and 33.4 meters in height; the outer face has a single-eave hip-gable roof, the inner face a triple-tier roof with veranda, all in blue-grey round and flat tiles. Its frame is carried directly by the eave columns, while the king posts rise only to the third-highest beam and do not reach the ridge purlin; short beams tie the king posts to the eave columns fore and aft, with full seven-rafter beams only at the two central bays. Four interior levels with timber stairs let soldiers reach the embrasures: on the front face four rows of twelve openings each (two in the central bay), forty-eight in all; on each flank three rows of three, eighteen in all — sixty-six openings altogether. The tower is brick-faced on three sides. Both main and arrow towers have open, exposed roof frames, their hip-gable corners worked either with diagonal beams combined with longitudinal and tie beams, or with longitudinal beams alone and no diagonal beams — differences likely arising from repairs of different periods.

Because the wall stands well above ground, ramps were needed to move between ground and rampart. The Ming city had eleven such ramps: seven along the inner side of the circuit and four to the left of the gate towers inside the four gates (another account gives six along the wall, three surviving). The seven inner ramps are brick-paved rolling slopes, with a 1.5-meter stepped walk on the inner right side for infantry; the ramp rises at 25 degrees, is six meters wide, reaches level with the crown pavement, and hugs the inner face of the wall as a double-flight isosceles trapezoid 100 meters along its base and 10 meters at the top. A guard wall flanks each ramp, its lower entrance fitted with a gate barrier, the gate lacquered red — the "great red gate" — and manned by troops. In addition to the ramp at each gate tower's left, six more ran along the circuit, each about 100 meters long and six meters wide. Barbicans also hold ramps to the wall top, gently sloped without steps so that horses could be ridden up.

Each of the four main gates has its own history. Yongning, the south gate, is the oldest and longest in continuous use; founded in the second year of Kaihuang, Sui (582), it was originally Anshang men, the eastern of the imperial city's three south gates, retained as the south gate when Han Jian shrank the city in the late Tang, and renamed Yongning in the Ming. Anding, the west gate, was originally Shunyi men, the central gate on the west of the Tang imperial city, likewise preserved by Han Jian; the Ming shifted its position slightly south and gave it its present name, signifying peace and stability on the western frontier. It is the most completely preserved ancient castle in China; conservators discovered within its early-Ming interior some 1,000 square meters of painting surviving over six centuries, vital material for the study of Ming painting technique and architectural art history. That so much survived is owing to the main tower's comparatively single use — a military command post, the "headquarters" of the Ming northwestern command — unlike the arrow towers' combat role. Anyuan, the north gate, was the principal reception gate in the Tang, called "the foremost gate under heaven"; its name expresses the central court's policy of conciliation toward distant peoples. Its tower was burned in the 1911 revolution. Changle, the east gate, prays that the Great Ming might long enjoy felicity; in the sixteenth year of Chongzhen (1643), not yet 270 years after its building, it was burned by Li Zicheng's rebel army and rebuilt in the Qing. The three characters of its lintel were written in the sixth month of the first year of the Republic (1912) by a Shaanxi military governor surnamed Zhang; before the Xi'an Incident, Zhang Xueliang organized training and student units in the tower above the east gate, a site now restored as an Xi'an Incident memorial. Beyond these, the south gate's main tower was destroyed by artillery fire in the 1911 revolution and its arrow tower burned in 1926; the four sluice towers and crescent enclosures were demolished between the late Qing and the early Republic, the north main tower and south arrow tower burning in 1911 and 1926 respectively. Six main and arrow towers survive: the south main tower; the west main and arrow towers; the north arrow tower; and the east main and arrow towers.

From the Republican period onward, gates were opened to ease movement in and out of the old city: Zhongshan men (1927), Yuxiang men, Jiefang men (1934, originally Zhongzheng men), Wumu men (1939), Jianguo men, Heping men (1953), Wenchang men (1986), Zhuque men (1986), Hanguang men, and Chaoyang men (1994), among others — eighteen gates in all today (one account gives sixteen arched openings and breaches). Notable among them: in 1984, during restoration, the Hanguang men site was excavated, revealing granite column bases and a carved threshold and passage; the new arched gate was placed east of the remains, which were protected within a frame structure and faced with brick to match the wall, with artificial lighting and climate control installed — the Tang Imperial City Hanguang Gate Site Museum. In 1985 the Zhuque men site, encased within the Ming wall, was excavated and the gate reopened in 1986. The wall also carries one structure with no military purpose: the Kuixing Pavilion, built on the wall beside the Ming-Qing Xi'an prefectural school and Confucian temple (today's Stele Forest museum); repeatedly damaged over the centuries, it was restored in 1986, and the gate opened beneath it was named Wenchang men. Tang wall and gate remains have been found within both the west and south walls during restorations old and recent. Between 1983 and 1991 the Ministry of Finance and the Shaanxi provincial and Xi'an municipal governments invested over 200 million yuan in a comprehensive restoration: besides repairing brickwork and the gate towers and corner platforms, they reconstructed one Kuixing Pavilion and ten defensive towers and built a ring park along the moat. In 1991 the crescent enclosure, sluice tower, and drawbridge were rebuilt outside the south gate's barbican, and in 2013 the south arrow tower was reconstructed. Jiefang men — originally Zhongzheng men — was opened in 1934 when the Longhai Railway reached Xi'an; in 1952 a 516-meter breach was cut to expand the station square. In 2005 the gap was reconnected with three arch-like gate openings totaling 200 meters in span — 84 meters at the center and 58 on each side — whereupon the wall became continuous once more.

韩城司马迁祠

Sima Qian Memorial Temple, Hancheng

级别：全国重点文物保护单位（1982年第二批） 年代：始建西晋永嘉四年（310），北宋宣和七年（1125）奠定规模，明清修葺 地址：渭南市韩城市芝川镇韩奕坡悬崖上 类型：纪念祠庙·宋代木构遗存（纪念史圣司马迁）

司马迁，字子长，西汉夏阳（今陕西韩城市南，一说山西河津）人，生于汉景帝中元五年（前145年，或作前135年）。他继父司马谈之职任太史令，因替败降匈奴的李陵仗义执言触怒汉武帝，惨遭宫刑；《史记》未完，他不肯就死，忍辱发愤，历时十余年撰成中国第一部纪传体通史《史记》——上起轩辕黄帝、下至汉武帝太初年间约三千年史事，凡十二本纪、三十世家、七十列传、十表、八书一百三十篇、五十二万余言。鲁迅誉之为"史家之绝唱，无韵之离骚"，后世尊其为"史圣"。司马迁祠即建在他的故乡，是祭祀这位"中华史圣"的专祠，与其墓冢合称司马迁祠墓。因地处黄河西岸高岗、当地又称"太史高坟"；1982年2月23日被国务院公布为第二批全国重点文物保护单位，2014年评为国家四A级景区，清代起列入"关中八景"之"太史高坟"。

司马迁祠的始建年代已不可考。据郦道元《水经注》所记，西晋永嘉四年（310年）的一次修葺是现有文献中最早的记录——是年汉阳太守殷济瞻仰遗文、感其功德，遂为司马迁建石室、立碑树桓。此后历代沿续：北宋宣和七年（1125年）由韩城县令尹阳主持增修扩建，奠定今日祠庙规模；经金、元、明三代各有小修，其中明代三次修葺（1438、1536、1606年）规模皆不大；清代则有三次大修——康熙六十一年（1722年）、乾隆十四年（1749年，相距二十七年）、光绪十二年（1886年）。又因韩城地方文物部门为抢救濒危古建，上世纪七八十年代先后将境内的彰耀寺大殿、三圣庙、禹王庙大殿等迁入祠内，形成了今天祠墓加多座迁建殿宇的格局。祠内保存古今碑石一百余通，内容涉及司马迁人品、祠墓历史变迁、修缮祭祀、历代文人咏赞及《史记》名言警句，其中西晋《司马庙全图》、明《汉太史公世系碑》、清康熙《宋和宁题诗》等尤为珍贵。

司马迁祠选址极见匠心。它坐落在韩城市南约十公里芝川镇韩奕坡（司马坡）的悬崖上，东临滔滔黄河，西枕巍巍梁山，凭高俯下，选地绝胜。祠的营造者凭借天然陡峻的地势与雄伟的河山景色，意图创造一种引人崇敬的肃穆气氛——"高山仰止，景行行止"，构祠以祀，正借此体现司马迁的高风亮节。建筑群坐西朝东，顺着山势分四层而建，层层升高，各层之间以石阶相接。坡下东北方先见一座木牌坊，清光绪十二年（1886）建，正面匾书"汉太史司马祠"（一说"汉太史司马迁祠墓"，为已故书法大家启功所书）。由此上行，过司马古道——这条古道始建于春秋、秦统一后为韩城通长安的要道，因祠建于此改称司马古道，现存北宋年间的石条历经八百余年雨水冲刷与车马碾轧，已凹凸不平，石上深深的辙印既见证昔日繁华，又仿佛昭示太史公一生的坎坷。登至岔道口，遇"高山仰止"木牌坊，语出《诗经·小雅·车辖》，为韩城书法家强汉三所书，寓意司马迁人格如山、令人景仰。从此迤逦而上，又是一座书着"河山之阳"的砖砌牌坊——"河山之阳"出自《太史公自序》，指太史公的出生之地、归葬之所。

过牌坊便是通往祠院的"朝圣道"，共九十九级台阶——九为阳数之极，九十九级象征最高礼制规格。这条上祠之道，是清康熙七年（1668年）韩城县令翟世琪率领芝川民众大规模扩建司马迁祠时，将数丈深的沟填平修筑而成。拾阶而上，先见一座砖砌山门，上书"史笔昭世"（一说此砖坊额书"河山之阳"，两侧对联"圣人光道统，汉史竞经文"，将《史记》与儒家五经相媲美）；山门上的"龙门才子故里"砖坊今日已毁、仅存遗址。走完九十九级台阶，进入祠院，大门（即山门）额上悬"太史祠"三字，遒劲古拙，雄气逼人。祠院四周有砖砌高大墙垛，院内古柏参天、青烟袅袅，坐西朝东居中为献殿，是举行祭祀、陈列祭器之地。

献殿面阔五间、进深三间（一说进深两间），悬山顶过堂式，建筑面积约一百三十四平方米，是北宋宣和七年由韩城县令尹阳主持建造的宋代原构。殿正中木匾上书"文史祖宗"四字，是对司马迁历史地位的至高评价；两边木刻楹联为"刚直不阿，留得正气凌霄汉；幽而发愤，著成信史照尘寰"，相传为司马迁后裔所献，上联赞其不畏强权、为李陵仗义执言，下联颂其忍辱发愤、撰写《史记》。殿内碑碣林立，共六十六通——宋代四通、金代五通、元代一通、明代二十一通、清代三十四通、新中国一通。1958年祠墓整修竣工后，曾邀郭沫若题诗勒石："龙门有灵秀，钟毓人中龙；学殖空前富，文章旷代雄；怜才膺斧钺，吐气作霓虹；功业追尼父，千秋太史公。"

献殿之后为寝殿，面阔三间、平面长方形，建筑面积约一百零四平方米，与献殿前檐仅隔一线天沟，留出前院，周以垣墙，围成一座完整院落，院内两株苍劲古柏更添肃穆。寝殿重修于北宋靖康元年（1126年），当心间面阔三点六米、通进深约八米，其屋架椽、梁、斗拱、檐等结构、风格与材制均为宋代无疑。殿内正中筑砖龛，龛上书"穆然清风"四字；龛内设幔帐，供奉宋代司马迁泥塑彩像一尊，像高一百四十厘米，方脸、黑须三绺飘胸，端庄正坐、面稍北顾，身着红袍、手挟玉带，仪表巍然——相传司马迁夫人擅画，曾为其夫画多幅肖像，宋代塑像时由后人献出，匠人依画像而塑，故所表现的是司马迁的真实神态。寝殿与献殿现存建筑的风格简朴，一式灰瓦悬山顶，作博风、悬鱼、惹草；木作不施彩绘，规模体量也不大。其气势之雄伟，并不在于建筑本身，而在于周围的自然环境与所踞的地势，更在于建筑与自然、地势的结合。

墓冢在祠院之后（寝殿右侧有通道可出入），是砖砌的圆形蒙古包式冢，四周垂直、顶为穹庐形，冢高二点一五米、周长十三点一九米，系元代修建（经西晋、宋、金、元、清五次修葺）。墓壁周围嵌有砖雕八卦图案和花卉图案十六幅，墓四周有砖垣围护。冢上古柏一株，树分五枝、枝干苍劲、盘若蛟龙，称"五子登科柏"，传为汉代所植——古制以为柏树气味可御"魍象"怪物，故墓冢植柏，而此柏又添登科及第的祥兆，每年高考前多有学子前来祭拜。墓前石碑刻"汉太史司马公墓"，为清乾隆年间陕西巡抚兼都察院右副都御史毕沅所题（一说题于乾隆四十一年1776年，一说四十七年1782年）。祠后寝殿右侧立有清康熙御碑，为全国唯二祭奠司马迁的御碑之一。

司马迁祠的祠院现存建筑中，以山门和寝殿较为古老，其大木结构仍保留某些宋代做法特征，为陕西省内一处很有价值的古建筑。山门面阔一间、进深两椽；构架为前后搭牵、分心用三柱；屋坡举折平缓，约为四比一（1:4）；前后檐斗拱四铺作出单抄，补间用一朵，于顺脊串上施单材襻间。寝殿面阔三间、进深四椽、带前廊；构架为搭牵对三椽栿用三柱；三椽栿上用驼峰，平梁上用叉手、蜀柱，安丁华抹颏拱，于柱头缝施单材襻间；角柱做生起、侧脚；柱为梭柱；前檐柱头斗拱四铺作出单抄，补间用一朵；当心间、补间四铺作外插昂，昂下刻作华头子；内檐出单抄，耍头后尾起挑斡向上交于平槫下——这诸多手法皆具宋《营造法式》的做法特征；又于令拱左右出琴面昂，属一种地方手法。这些"分心用三柱""搭牵对三椽栿""琴面昂""耍头后尾起挑斡"等专业术语，正是古建测绘语汇的精准呈现，说明司马迁祠的山门与寝殿是研究宋《营造法式》的珍贵实例。

Sima Qian, courtesy name Zichang, was born in Xiayang of the Western Han (south of present-day Hancheng, Shaanxi, some sources say Hejin, Shanxi) in 145 BC (or 135 BC). Succeeding his father Sima Tan as Grand Historian (taishi ling), he came to grief for speaking in defense of the defeated general Li Ling and was castrated by order of the wrathful Emperor Wu; rather than die before his work was done, he endured the shame and over more than a decade wrote China's first comprehensive history in the biographical-annal form, the Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji) — some 520,000 characters in 130 chapters covering roughly three millennia from the Yellow Emperor to the early Han. Lu Xun called it "the swan song of historians, an unrhymed Li Sao"; posterity honors him as the Sage of History. The memorial temple at his native place, with his tomb, is the Hancheng shrine to this "Sage of History," known locally as the "high grave of the Grand Historian." On 23 February 1982 the State Council listed it among the second batch of National Key Protected Heritage Sites, and in 2014 it became a national 4A scenic area.

The date of the temple's first founding is unknown. According to Li Daoyuan's Commentary on the Water Classic, the earliest record is a repair in the fourth year of Yongjia of the Western Jin (310), when Yin Ji, governor of Hanyang, admiring Sima Qian's writings and moved by his merit, raised a stone chamber and set up a stele and gates. Thereafter the temple grew steadily: in the seventh year of Xuanhe of the Northern Song (1125) the county magistrate Yin Yang directed an enlargement that established the present scale; minor works followed under Jin, Yuan, and Ming (three Ming repairs, in 1438, 1536, and 1606, were all small); and the Qing carried out three great repairs, in 1722, 1749, and 1886. Because Hancheng's cultural authorities sought to rescue endangered buildings, from the 1970s onward the great halls of the Zhangyao Temple, the Sansheng Temple, and the Yu Temple were moved here, giving the complex its present form. Over a hundred ancient and modern steles within record Sima Qian's character, the temple's vicissitudes, its repairs and sacrifices, verses of praise, and maxims from the Shiji; the Western Jin Map of the Sima Temple, the Ming Genealogical Stele of the Grand Historian, and the Kangxi-era poem stele of Song Hening are among the most prized.

The temple's siting is masterly. It rests on the cliff of Hanyi Slope (Sima Slope) in Zhichuan Town, some ten kilometers south of Hancheng, facing east toward the vast Yellow River and backed by the rugged Liang Mountains. Its builders exploited the natural steepness and the grandeur of river and mountain to create a solemn, awe-inspiring atmosphere — "high the mountain, and one looks up to it" (shangao yangzhi) — raising the shrine to honor Sima Qian's lofty moral bearing. The complex faces east and climbs the slope in four terraces, linked by stone steps. At the foot, a timber archway of 1886 bears "Han Taishi Sima Ci" (the Shrine of the Grand Historian of Han); beyond it runs the ancient Sima Road, laid in the Spring and Autumn period and the main route from Hancheng to Chang'an after Qin unification, its surviving Northern Song slabs worn uneven by eight centuries of rain and wheel, the deep ruts both testimony to past bustle and emblem of the historian's own arduous path. At a fork stands the wooden archway "Gao Shan Yang Zhi," from the Book of Songs, written by the Hancheng calligrapher Qiang Hansan, signifying Sima Qian's mountain-like virtue; farther on a brick archway is inscribed "He Shan Zhi Yang" (the sunny side of river and mountain), from Sima Qian's own autobiographical epilogue, naming his birthplace and final resting place.

Beyond these rises the "Pilgrim's Way" of ninety-nine steps — nine being the utmost yang number, the count marking the highest ritual dignity. The way was built in 1668 when Zhai Shiqi, magistrate of Hancheng, led the people of Zhichuan in a great enlargement of the temple, filling a ravine several zhang deep. The brick gate at its head bears "Shi Bi Zhao Shi" (the brush of history illumines the age); the brick archway inscribed "Longmen caizi guli" (native place of the scholar of the Dragon Gate) has perished, its site alone remaining. At the top, the temple gate hangs a plaque reading "Tai Shi Ci," rugged and powerful. Within the high brick walls, ancient cypresses stand in solemn rows and incense drifts; facing east at the center stands the Sacrifice Hall (xiandian), where offerings were laid and rites performed.

The Sacrifice Hall is five bays wide and three deep, a hip-gable pass-through hall of some 134 square meters, built in the Northern Song by Magistrate Yin Yang in 1125. At its center hangs the plaque "Wen Shi Zu Zong" (Ancestor of Literature and History), the supreme tribute to Sima Qian's standing, flanked by the couplet "Just and unbending, his righteous spirit pierces the Milky Way; hidden away, yet inspired, he wrote the true history that shines upon the world," said to have been offered by Sima Qian's own descendants. Within stand sixty-six steles — four Song, five Jin, one Yuan, twenty-one Ming, thirty-four Qing, and one from the People's Republic. In 1958, when the temple's restoration was completed, Guo Moruo was invited to write a poem, cut into stone, in praise of Sima Qian.

Behind the Sacrifice Hall stands the Sleeping Hall (qindian), three bays wide over a rectangular plan of some 104 square meters, separated from the Sacrifice Hall's front eave only by a narrow sky-well, its front court enclosed by walls into a complete courtyard shaded by two ancient cypresses. The hall was rebuilt in the first year of Jingkang of the Northern Song (1126); its central bay is 3.6 meters wide and some 8 meters deep, and the structure, style, and timber grades of its rafters, beams, brackets, and eaves are unmistakably Song. Within, a brick niche inscribed "Mu Ran Qing Feng" (Serene and Pure as a Breeze) holds a Song clay polychrome seated image of Sima Qian, 140 centimeters tall — square-faced, with a black three-strand beard flowing over his chest, seated upright and slightly turned, robed in red with a jade belt, dignified in bearing. Tradition holds that Sima Qian's wife was a painter and made portraits of her husband, which descendants offered when the statue was modeled in Song times, so that it shows his true features. Both hall and Sacrifice Hall are simple in style — grey-tiled hip-gable roofs with bofeng, xuanyu, and recao finials, unpainted timber, modest in scale. The grandeur lies not in the buildings themselves but in the surrounding nature and the commanding site, and in the marriage of the two.

Behind the courtyard (reached by a passage right of the Sleeping Hall) stands the tomb — a round brick mound of Mongol-yurt form, vertical on all sides with a domed top, 2.15 meters high and 13.19 meters in circumference, built in the Yuan and repaired five times from the Western Jin through the Song, Jin, Yuan, and Qing. Sixteen panels of brick relief, of trigram and floral motifs, ring the burial, enclosed by a brick wall. On the mound grows a single ancient cypress with five branches winding like a coiling dragon, the "five-sons-success cypress," said to be planted in the Han — cypress was planted about tombs because its scent wards off the man-eating wangxiang sprite, and this tree adds the good omen of five sons passing the examinations, so students come to pray before it each spring. Before the tomb a stele reads "Han Taishi Sima Gong Mu" (the Tomb of the Grand Historian of Han, Lord Sima), written by Bi Yuan, governor-general of Shaanxi under the Qianlong emperor (variously dated 1776 or 1782).

Of the temple's surviving structures, the gate and the Sleeping Hall are the oldest, and their great timber frames still show certain Song mannerisms, making the temple a valuable piece of early building in Shaanxi. The gate is one bay wide and two rafters deep, its frame of front-and-back tie beams divided by three columns (fenxin yong san zhu); its roof pitch is gentle, about one in four; the eave bracket sets are four-tier with a single cantilever, one set between bays, with a single-member puan set upon the ridge purlin. The Sleeping Hall is three bays wide and four rafters deep with a front veranda; its frame pairs tie beams against a three-rafter beam on three columns; a camel's hump (tuofeng) crowns the three-rafter beam, while the flat beam bears forked struts (chashou) and a short column (shuzhu), with a dinghua mo'e arch fitted and single-member puan at the column-head line; the corner columns rise and batter; the columns are spindle-shaped; the front eave bracket sets are four-tier with a single cantilever, one set between bays; the central and intermediate sets are four-tier with projecting ang whose undersides are carved as huatouzi; within, the eaves issue a single cantilever and the tail of the shuatou rises as a tiaoo turning upward to meet the flat purlin — many of these manners are those of the Song building manual Yingzao Fashi — while the qinmian ang on either side of the ling gong is a local idiom. These technical terms — fenxin yong san zhu, daqian dui sanchuan fu, qinmian ang, and the rising tails of the shuatou — mark the gate and Sleeping Hall as valuable instances for the study of the Yingzao Fashi.
