蓟州独乐寺

Dule Temple (Temple of Solitary Joy), Jizhou

级别：全国重点文物保护单位（1961年第一批） 年代：始建于隋，辽统和二年（984）重建山门、观音阁 地址：天津市蓟州区武定街41号 类型：辽代寺院·早期木构建筑（中国现存三大辽代寺院之一）

独乐寺位于天津蓟州区城内的武定街，因寺在蓟县（蓟州旧称渔阳）古城而名，俗称大佛寺。它是中国现存三大辽代寺院之一，与山西大同华严寺、辽宁义县奉国寺并列，也是"八大辽构"中现存最早的实例。寺的始建年代虽无确证，却至迟在唐初已经创立；现存的观音阁与山门为辽圣宗统和二年（984）重建，是当时蓟州僧人谈真在唐初旧基上兴建的。寺名"独乐"三说并存：一谓寺西北有独乐水，寺以水名；一谓寺内大佛"独以普度众生为乐"；而流传最广的一说，是唐代安禄山在此起兵叛唐前誓师，"思独乐而不与民同乐"，故自命寺名——这座古寺，竟与"渔阳鼙鼓动地来"的安史之乱有着历史的牵连。1961年3月4日，独乐寺与北京故宫等一同被国务院公布为第一批全国重点文物保护单位，编号1-0084，是天津最早入选"国保"的建筑，2004年又列为国家四A级景区、"津门十景"之一。

独乐寺的伽蓝布局今已非旧貌——隋唐之制本是"寺分数院、周绕回廊"，自清乾隆十八年（1753）重修并将建筑偏于东院后，原有规模更变，原物仅存山门与观音阁。寺院坐北朝南，平面长方形，南北长一百五十米、东西宽一百一十米，占地约一万六千五百平方米。1932年4月，建筑学家梁思成率中国营造学社成员前来考察测绘，随后发表《蓟县独乐寺观音阁山门考》，刊于《中国营造学社汇刊》第三卷第二期"独乐寺专号"——这是营造学社第一次以"专号"形式发表的测绘报告，也被学界视为中国近代古建筑研究的奠基之作之一。梁思成在书中对这座建筑给予极高评价，称它"上承唐代遗风，下启宋式营造，实研究我国建筑蜕变上之重要资料，罕有之宝物也"，并说"熟悉敦煌壁画中净土图者，若骤见此阁，必疑身之已入西方极乐世界矣"。

山门是入寺的第一座建筑，面阔三间、进深二间，明间南北贯通作穿堂，是中国现存最早的庑殿顶山门——庑殿顶即四阿顶，是明清最高等级的屋顶形制，与故宫太和殿同级。山门台基平面长方形，长十九点一八米、宽十三点二二米，四周施压阑石，西北角尚存一块凸雕卧狮的曲尺形角石。大木构架为四架椽屋、分心用三柱通檐栿，前后檐柱共十根、分心置中柱两根，中柱高四点三四米、角柱高四点四二米，檐柱有明显的"侧脚"与"生起"，使整座建筑稳重而不僵硬。柱头斗栱五铺作重抄偷心造，补间铺作每间一朵，立于阑额正中的直枓之上——这种"直枓"用法是中国现存唐、宋建筑实物中的孤例；转角斗栱中的抹角拱（四十五度斜拱），也是中国现存木结构中使用斜拱的最早实例。单檐庑殿顶举折平缓、出檐深远如翼，正脊两端的高大鸱吻翘起内转、口吞正脊，高一点三四米，据考为唐末五代原物，是中国现存建筑屋顶上最早的鸱尾实例。山门当心间檐下悬挂"独乐寺"三字匾额，相传为明代权相严嵩手书；门道两旁各立一尊辽代泥塑金刚力士像（俗称"哼哈二将"），东侧高四点五米、西侧高四点四五米，肌肉夸张、横眉怒目，线条粗犷雄健；两梢间后墙上是清代所绘的四大天王壁画。梁思成特别指出，山门柱径肥短、权衡稳重，"较清式所呈现象为稳固"，与敦煌唐风壁画中的建筑极为相似。

过山门即观音阁——这是独乐寺的绝对主角，也是中国现存最古老的木结构楼阁式建筑。观音阁重建于辽统和二年，面阔五间、进深四间，外观两层（下层有腰檐、平坐，上层单檐），实则"明二暗三"，中间藏有一层暗层，通高二十三米。平面柱网为宋《营造法式》所称的"殿堂"结构"金厢斗底槽"式，外檐柱十八根、内柱十根，共二十八根立柱，分内外两圈升起，柱间用明栿、草栿联结成双环框架，形成六边形井口以容纳高耸的观音像。柱身仅端部有卷杀并设侧脚，上下层柱以"叉柱造"相接——上层与暗层的檐柱都向内收进约半个柱径，使外观稳如磐石；暗层柱间又加斜撑，大大增强了整体刚度。全阁梁、柱、斗、枋数以千计，而用材却高度标准化：梁枋不下千百，大小只有六种，梁之高宽多为二比一，与现代工程合理的比例暗合。阁的斗栱是它最惊人的成就——依机能不同，或承檐、或承平坐、或承梁枋，或在柱头、或转角、或补间，内外上下各不相同，总计二十四种、一百五十二朵，梁思成称它"诚可谓集斗拱之大成者"，是中国木构斗栱演变的关键一环。观音阁与山门的瓦当鸱吻等屋面虽非原物，其主体木构却是辽代原构，历经唐山等三十余次大地震而始终不倒——1976年唐山大地震波及蓟县，独乐寺周围民房多毁，观音阁仅受到很小损害，其刚柔相济的结构（刚性的斗栱层与柔性的立柱层）经受了千年考验，被公认为与应县木塔齐名的杰出高层木构设计。

阁内最为人称道的，是那尊直抵三层天花藻井的辽代十一面观音像。塑像立于下层正中莲花座上，像高十五点四米，连同佛座共高十六点零八米，是中国现存最高大的室内泥塑站像；其头顶塑有十个小观音头像，加本尊共十一面，故称十一面观音。观音左臂下垂、双手作接引状，下着彩绘长裙，赤足立于莲台，法相庄严慈祥，虽是辽代重塑，却仍具盛唐造像的余韵。两侧各侍立一尊辽代胁侍菩萨，束发谦恭，亦为辽塑珍品。像后塑一尊面朝后方的"倒坐"海岛观音，为明代作品。东侧金柱上悬立一尊高约一点三米的"伽蓝神"关羽，西侧悬护法韦驮——这些造像与山门的哼哈二将同属辽代，同类作品在国内极为罕见。观音阁下层四壁绘有以十六罗汉和二明王为主题的壁画，间绘神变故事与重修信士像，全长四十五点三五米、高三点一五米、总面积达一百四十二点八五平方米，铁线描兼兰叶描，线条流畅，为元代绘制、明代重描。这批壁画能保存至今，全赖乾隆十八年那次大修——当时不知何故用白灰将壁画整体涂抹覆盖，反使它们在清人粉饰下"雪藏"了两百余年，直到二十世纪七十年代维修时才偶然发现白灰之下的原画，因祸得福。阁上层檐下悬挂"观音之阁"匾额，其"阁"字下缀"太白"二字小款，学界多据郭沫若《李白与杜甫》考证，认定是唐天宝十二年（753）李白北游幽蓟时手书——此匾传承有序、艺术水准高，曾入选中国百块名匾；下层檐下"具足圆成"匾则为清咸丰八年（1859）咸丰帝驾幸独乐寺时所书。

观音阁之后为韦驮亭，一座小巧的六角攒尖亭，亭内供奉一尊双手合十而立的韦驮铜像，姿态奇特（其标志性的金刚杵遍寻不见，学界多以为法物失散）。再北是四合院式的报恩院，始建于明代、清乾隆年间重建，前殿居中供大肚弥勒，两侧列中国历史上著名的"四大疯僧"像，神态各异、全国罕见；主殿大雄宝殿供奉横三世佛，东西配殿分别奉文殊、普贤——这种以弥勒院、报恩院自成一体的格局，与整个寺院构成罕见的"寺中寺"形制，使独乐寺成为一座显密俱全的道场。寺院东侧还有乾隆十八年（1753）为清帝谒陵途中驻跸而建的行宫，主殿迴廊内珍藏二十八块乾隆御笔碑刻、载诗文一百零七篇，被称为"乾隆书法艺术博物馆"。1972年大修时在观音阁内发现壁画，1998年维修又在阁顶发现唐代莲花勾头，印证了独乐寺始建于唐代的历史。如今的独乐寺，山门的鸱吻还带着唐末的雄浑，观音阁的斗栱还悬着辽代的气象，阁内那座十米多高的观音还低眉垂目——千年以来它独乐其间，成为中国木构建筑"上承唐风、下启宋制"最珍贵的活化石。

Dule Temple — the Temple of Solitary Joy — stands on Wuding Street in the old city of Jizhou (in ancient times Yuyang), the seat of Tianjin's Jizhou District, and is known colloquially as the Great Buddha Temple. It is one of China's three surviving Liao-dynasty monasteries, alongside Huayan Temple in Datong, Shanxi, and Fengguo Temple in Yi County, Liaoning, and the earliest extant example among the "eight great Liao structures." Though the date of its founding is unconfirmed, it existed at latest in the early Tang; its present Guanyin Pavilion and mountain gate were rebuilt in the second year of Tonghe of the Liao (984) by the Jizhou monk Tanzhen on old Tang foundations. Three accounts of the name "Dule" (solitary joy) survive: that a Dule River west of the temple gave it its name; that the great Buddha within found joy alone in saving all beings; and, most widely told, that the rebel general An Lushan swore his oath here before rising against the Tang in 755, naming the temple for his own "joy alone, refusing to share it with the people" — binding this ancient monastery to the drums of the An Lushan Rebellion. On 4 March 1961 the State Council listed Dule Temple among the first batch of National Key Protected Heritage Sites, the earliest in Tianjin so honored; in 2004 it was made a national 4A scenic spot, one of the "Ten Views of Tianjin."

The temple's original plan no longer survives — the Sui-Tang convention was a monastery of separate courts ringed by cloisters, and after the great restoration of 1753, which shifted the buildings east, the old arrangement was lost; of original fabric only the mountain gate and the pavilion remain. The temple faces south on a rectangular plan 150 meters north-south by 110 meters east-west, covering some 16,500 square meters. In April 1932 the architect Liang Sicheng brought the members of the Society for Research in Chinese Architecture (the Yingzao Xueshe) to survey it, and the following report, "A Study of the Guanyin Pavilion and Gate of the Dule Temple at Jixian," published as the "Dule Temple special issue" of the Society's journal — the first issue ever to bear the name "special issue" — is regarded as one of the founding works of modern research on Chinese ancient architecture. Liang prized the temple as standing "between the Tang style above and the Song construction below — rare treasure indeed, essential material for the study of the transformation of Chinese architecture," and observed that "one familiar with the Pure Land scenes in Dunhuang wall paintings, suddenly coming upon this pavilion, would think he had entered the Western Paradise."

The mountain gate, the first structure one enters, is three bays wide and two deep, its central bay pierced as a passage, and is the earliest surviving temple gate with a hip-gable (wudian) roof — the most exalted roof form of the Ming-Qing, the same grade as the roof of the Forbidden City's Hall of Supreme Harmony. Its platform is 19.18 meters by 13.22, ringed with stone edging, with one L-shaped corner stone carved with a recumbent lion surviving at the northwest corner. The frame, four-rafter and divided by two central columns under a through-beam, has ten eave columns and two central columns (4.34 meters tall) beside four corner columns (4.42 meters), all with marked batter (cejiao) and rise (shengqi). The bracket sets at the column heads are five-tier double-cantilever, open-core; between the columns one set per bay stands on a straight strut (zhidou) — a use of the straight strut unique among surviving Tang and Song structures — and the chamfered brackets at the corners are the earliest use of the diagonal bracket (mujiao gong) in surviving Chinese timberwork. The gently pitched single hip roof overhangs far and deep like wings, and the two great owl's-tail finials (chiwen) that grip the ridge, 1.34 meters high and believed to be late-Tang originals, are the earliest finials surviving on a Chinese building roof. Beneath the gate's central eave hangs the plaque "Dule Si," said to be the hand of the Ming grand secretary Yan Song; flanking the passage stand two Liao clay guardian kings (the "heng" and "ha" generals), 4.5 and 4.45 meters tall, with bulging muscle and glaring eyes; behind them on the rear walls are Qing paintings of the Four Heavenly Kings. Liang noted that the gate's fat, short columns look far more solid than Qing work, and that both gate and pavilion echo closely the Tang buildings seen in Dunhuang murals.

Beyond the gate rises the Guanyin Pavilion — the temple's true glory and the oldest surviving timber tower in China. Rebuilt in 984, it is five bays wide and four deep, appearing to be two stories (with a mezzanine eave and balustrade below the upper) but actually containing three, a hidden structural story between, and stands 23 meters tall. Its column plan is that which the Song building manual Yingzao Fashi calls the "treasure-hall" jinxiang doudi cao scheme: eighteen outer eave columns and ten inner columns, twenty-eight in all, rising in two rings bound by exposed and concealed beams into a double frame, leaving a hexagonal well at the heart to receive the towering Guanyin image. The columns are gently tapered at top and battered, and the upper and hidden stories' eave columns step inward about half a column's diameter onto those below by the "forked-column" (chazhu) method, giving an impression of rocklike stability; diagonal braces in the hidden story further stiffen the frame. Across the pavilion the thousands of beams, columns, brackets, and purlins are reduced to a remarkable standardization — the members come in only six sizes, with beams about two to one in depth to width, a proportion close to modern engineering ideal. But it is the bracket sets that crown the achievement: each differs by function — bearing eave, balustrade, or beam; at column head, corner, or between columns, above and below — numbering twenty-four varieties and 152 individual sets. Liang called it "truly the summation of the dougong," a pivotal chapter in the evolution of Chinese timber joinery. Though the tiles and finials are not original, the timber bodies of gate and pavilion are genuine Liao fabric, and have withstood more than thirty earthquakes — in the 1976 Tangshan quake, which leveled much of the surrounding town, the pavilion suffered only slight damage. Its structure, a rigid bracket layer above a flexible column layer, has proved itself across a millennium, and ranks with the Yingxian timber pagoda among the great high-rise timber designs of China.

Within the pavilion the celebrated centerpiece is the eleven-headed Guanyin of Liao date, rising to the very caisson ceiling. Set on a lotus throne, the statue is 15.4 meters tall, or 16.08 meters with its pedestal — the tallest indoor clay standing image in China. Ten small Guanyin heads crown its own, giving eleven faces, whence the name. Its left arm hangs, its hands make the gesture of welcome, and it stands barefoot on a lotus, serene and majestic, still bearing the spirit of High Tang sculpture though modeled in the Liao. Two Liao attendant bodhisattvas flank it; behind is a "reverse-facing" island Guanyin of Ming date. A "temple guardian" Guan Yu hangs 1.3 meters high on an eastern pillar and a Weituo on the west — these, with the gate's guardian kings, are Liao works, rarely matched anywhere. The lower walls of the pavilion bear murals of sixteen arhats and two vidyaraja kings, with miracle scenes and donor portraits, running 45.35 meters and 3.15 meters high over 142.85 square meters, drawn in iron-wire line with touches of orchid-leaf stroke, painted in the Yuan and repainted in the Ming. Their survival is owed to the very restoration of 1753, which — for reasons unknown — whitewashed them over, hiding them for more than two centuries until conservators in the 1970s chanced upon the paintings beneath the lime: harm that became blessing. On the pavilion's upper eave hangs the plaque "Guanyin zhi Ge," whose character ge carries the small signature "Taibai," leading most scholars, following Guo Moruo, to ascribe it to the poet Li Bai (whose hao was Taibai) during his northern journey of 753; the lower plaque, "Ju Zu Yuan Cheng," was brushed by the Xianfeng emperor when he visited in 1859.

Behind the pavilion stands the Weituo Pavilion, a small hexagonal kiosk sheltering a bronze Weituo with hands pressed together in prayer, his usual vajra staff conspicuously absent. Farther north the courtyard-style Baoyuan Cloister, founded in the Ming and rebuilt under Qianlong, enshrines a pot-bellied Maitreya in its front hall flanked by statues of the four celebrated "mad monks" of Chinese legend, each in a different pose, a rarity nationwide; its main hall holds the three Buddhas of past, present, and future, with Manjusri and Samantabhadra in side halls — a self-contained temple within the temple, making Dule a sanctuary complete in both exoteric and esoteric teaching. East of it, a 1753 traveling palace sheltered Qing emperors on their way to the eastern tombs; its corridor preserves twenty-eight imperial steles with one hundred and seven poems and essays by the Qianlong emperor, a "museum of Qianlong calligraphy." In 1972 a restoration revealed the murals within the pavilion; in 1998 further work on the roof uncovered Tang lotus tile-ends, confirming the temple's Tang origins. Today the gate's finials still bear the vigor of the late Tang, the pavilion's bracket sets the majesty of the Liao, and the ten-meter Guanyin still looks down with lowered brows — a living fossil of Chinese timber architecture, spanning the transition from Tang style to Song system.
